Back to work after a baby: what studies of over 10,000 children say about childcare, and how to choose without the guilt

You are packing a nursery bag for the first time, and part of you feels you are handing your baby away. The research is kinder than the voice in your head. Here is what the biggest studies found, how to pick good care, and how to make drop-off, feeding and the first winter easier.

If you feel guilty, you are in very large company

On Mumsnet, the paid childcare board is one of the busiest, at 643 pages of threads. In September 2026, a Mum wrote that she felt guilty about her baby growing up in childcare, and 61 replies followed. In October 2026, a thread about nursery settling going badly drew 62 replies.

Many parents there describe the same mix: relief at going back, then guilt about the relief. Experienced parents often reply that the hardest part was the first few weeks.

What the biggest studies found, and what they did not

StudyWhat it foundWhat it did not find
NICHD Study of Early Child Care, United States, from 1991, over 1,000 children at 10 sitesFamily features were 2 to 3 times more strongly linked to development than childcare. Higher-quality care was linked to somewhat better language, reading and maths, and at age 15 to higher achievement and fewer acting-out behaviours. Children in care 30 hours or more a week showed somewhat more problem behaviour in care and at school entry, and at 15 more impulsiveness and risk-taking.It was not designed to show cause and effect. The links with childcare were described as slight.
EPPSE, England, 1997 to 2014, 2,800 children from age 3 to 16Pre-school was linked to better results at school, still visible in exams at 16. High quality and 2 to 3 years of attendance gave the biggest gains. Part-time did as well as full-time at school entry.Pre-school did not replace home. Learning at home, such as reading and play, had a larger effect on maths at 16.
Norway, 2013, over 75,000 childrenSiblings who spent more time in childcare showed no more behaviour problems than siblings who spent less. Most children started around age 1.No sign that more hours caused more behaviour problems.
7 studies in 5 countries, 2022, 10,105 childrenGermany, the Netherlands, Norway, Canada and the US: almost no evidence that long hours in a centre cause behaviour problems.It looked only at the short term.
Norway's 1975 childcare expansion, ages 3 to 6Children who gained a place went on to more years of education and work as adults.It covered children aged 3 to 6, not babies.
Quebec, Canada, low-cost universal childcareThe honest warning: worse behaviour that lasted into school, and later worse health, lower life satisfaction and more crime.Little effect on test scores. The rise in crime was mainly in boys. This is the main study that points the other way.

A review of 69 studies from 1960 to 2010, published by the American Psychological Association (APA), found that children whose Mums went back to work before age 3 were no more likely to have school or behaviour problems overall. Children in single-parent or low-income families often did better. The review found that when Mums in better-off, 2-parent families worked full time in the first year, children later showed slightly more acting-out behaviour and slightly lower achievement.

The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) report Starting Strong VI (2021) sums up why quality decides so much. Children learn through their daily interactions with carers and other children. A warm, responsive carer is the heart of good care.

What Hoppo takes from this: good-quality care is safe and often helpful, and home matters most. If you can choose, pick warmth over polish, and consider shorter days in the first year. Long days are no reason for guilt.

The visit checklist: what to look for and what to ask

  • Ratios. Ask how many babies each adult cares for. US national standards recommend no more than 3 babies under 12 months per adult, in groups of no more than 6. England requires 1 adult for every 3 children under 2. Rules differ by country, so ask what applies at lunchtime and late in the day too.
  • A key person. Ask who will be your baby's main carer. In England every child in a group setting must have one. Anywhere, 1 familiar adult helps a baby settle.
  • Settling-in plan. Good settings offer short visits together, then short spells alone, building up gradually.
  • Safe sleep. Babies should sleep on their backs on a firm, flat mattress, with no pillows, bumpers, blankets or soft toys, and never in a car seat, swing or bouncer. Ask how often sleeping babies are checked. These rules follow the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), and the NHS gives the same core advice. See our safe sleep guide.
  • Sickness policy. When must your child stay home, and how soon do they call you about a fever?
  • Feeding. Will they feed expressed breast milk, follow your baby's cues, and store milk safely?
  • Your eyes. Do carers get down to the babies' level, talk to them and comfort crying quickly? Read the latest inspection report if there is one.

Drop-off: why a short goodbye is kinder

The AAP advises a short goodbye ritual, the same every day, then leaving. Once you have gone, do not come back unless you plan to stay. Never sneak out without saying goodbye, as it can undermine your child's trust (California Childcare Health Program).

  • Before 7 months, most babies adjust well to a consistent carer.
  • From about 7 to 12 months, stranger anxiety starts. Ease in gradually if you can.
  • Between 12 and 24 months, separation anxiety peaks, says the AAP. Crying at drop-off is common in the first weeks.
  • Do a trial run before your first work day, as the NHS suggests.
  • Bring a comfort item from home.

Expect tired, clingy evenings at first. Our starting daycare guide has more ideas.

Keeping breastfeeding going, if you want to

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends breastfeeding up to 2 years or beyond, alongside food from 6 months. Going back to work does not have to end it. Some parents express at work, some feed only morning and evening, and some mix in formula. All of these count.

The International Labour Organization (ILO) Maternity Protection Convention gives breastfeeding mothers the right to daily paid breaks or shorter hours, with details set by each country. A 2010 ILO report counted at least 92 countries with breastfeeding breaks in law. Check your own rights with your employer, union or government website.

  • Tell your employer before you return. In the UK, the NHS says to do this in writing.
  • Ask for a private, clean place to express, never a toilet (NHS).
  • Label milk with the date, so the carer uses the oldest first.

The 2 bodies differ on fridge time, and even 2 NHS pages give different limits. As a safe default, use the shortest times: 4 hours at room temperature, 4 days in the fridge (3 days if it is warmer than 4°C), 24 hours in a cool bag and 6 months in the freezer. Once your baby has drunk from a bottle, use it within 1 hour and throw away the rest. Use thawed milk straight away and never refreeze it (NHS, CDC).

The first-year colds: more now, fewer later

The AAP says most children have around 8 to 10 colds in their first 2 years. Close contact with other children adds more. A study of 1,238 families in Quebec, Canada, published in 2010, found that children who started large-group childcare before age 2 and a half had more infections early on, then fewer once they started school. Overall they were ill about as often. Children in small groups had no more infections than children at home.

Agree sick-day cover before you go back. Our colds guide explains what helps at home and when to get help.

About the guilt: 3 things worth remembering

  1. You are still the main event. In the US study, family life was 2 to 3 times more strongly linked to development than childcare. Your evenings, weekends and bedtime stories count.
  2. Your baby can love more than 1 adult. A warm carer adds to your bond. It does not replace it.
  3. Work can help your family. Income and your own wellbeing reach your child too.

The short version

  • Large studies find good-quality childcare is safe and often helpful. Family life matters most.
  • Quality means warm, steady carers, small groups and good ratios. Ask about them on every visit.
  • Check safe sleep, sickness rules and settling-in. Keep goodbyes short and the same every day.
  • More colds in the first year are expected, and plan sick-day cover early.

When to talk to your doctor or health visitor

  • Your child is still very upset at drop-off after several weeks, or stops eating or sleeping well.
  • Your baby is under 3 months and has a temperature of 38°C or higher. This is urgent. Call your doctor straight away, or 111 in the UK (NHS, AAP).
  • Call your emergency number if your child struggles to breathe, has pauses in breathing, has blue, grey or blotchy skin, lips or tongue, is floppy and hard to wake, has a fit, or has a rash that does not fade when you press a glass against it (NHS).
  • Your own mood is low most days, or worry about going back fills your thoughts. Our low mood guide explains where to start.

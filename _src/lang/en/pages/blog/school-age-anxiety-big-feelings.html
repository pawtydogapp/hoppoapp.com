3 in 4 children have fears. How to tell everyday worry from anxiety that needs help

Tummy aches on school mornings. Bedtime questions that never end. A child who will not go to a party without you. Most of this is normal and passes. Some of it is anxiety worth getting help for. Here is what the numbers say, how to tell the difference, 8 tools from the evidence and what to say tonight.

1 in 6 parents hear about tummy aches every month

In a US poll published on 19 June 2023, C.S. Mott Children's Hospital asked 1,061 parents of children aged 3 to 10 about belly pain. 17% said their child complains of it at least once a month. 27% put it down to worry and anxiety, avoiding school or seeking attention. Parents of 6 to 10 year olds were more likely to think so (34%) than parents of 3 to 5 year olds (20%). Read this parent poll as a signal, not a study.

The same questions come up on Mumsnet. In January 2025, a parent described a 6 year old who had developed separation anxiety, did not want to go to school and sometimes had tummy aches, which the doctor linked to worry about school. In September 2026, another parent asked how to help a son, just turned 6, who froze with panic when his morning routine ran a few minutes late. Both were read in November 2026.

Most fears are normal, and they change with age

A Maastricht University study from 2000 asked 190 ordinary children aged 4 to 12 about their fears. 75.8% reported fears, 67.4% reported worries and 80.5% reported scary dreams. The authors, led by Peter Muris, concluded that these anxiety symptoms are quite common.

Fears and scary dreams were common at 4 to 6, peaked at 7 to 9 and dropped at 10 to 12. Worry was more common in older children. Fear of imaginary creatures faded, while worry about tests grew.

Australia's government-funded Pregnancy, Birth and Baby service, run by Healthdirect, lists typical school-age fears: natural disasters, injury, illness, death and parents divorcing. The National Health Service (NHS) suggests explaining anxiety to children who are old enough as a natural reaction to stress, like a wave that builds up and then goes away again.

How common anxiety is: the numbers behind the headlines

The best data for this age come from NHS England's survey of children's mental health. In 2017 it assessed 3,597 children aged 5 to 10, using interviews with parents and teachers. 9.5% of them had at least 1 mental disorder. 4.1% had an emotional disorder, a group made up mostly of anxiety disorders.

A follow-up, published on 21 November 2023, used a screening questionnaire called the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ). It found a probable mental disorder in 15.7% of 8 to 10 year olds, with similar rates in boys (17.7%) and girls (13.6%). Among 8 to 16 year olds, the rate rose from 12.5% in 2017 to 20.3% in 2023.

Care is needed. "Probable disorder" is a questionnaire estimate, not a diagnosis, and it includes behaviour and attention problems. So 15.7% does not mean 1 in 6 has an anxiety disorder.

Worldwide, a 2015 meta-analysis of 41 studies from 27 countries put any anxiety disorder in children and teenagers at 6.5%. The World Health Organization (WHO) fact sheet of 1 September 2025 estimates that 4.1% of 10 to 14 year olds have an anxiety disorder. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reports that 11% of children aged 3 to 17 had diagnosed anxiety in 2023 to 2024.

A 2017 UK trial paper by Cathy Creswell and colleagues notes that half of all lifetime anxiety disorders emerge before age 12. So persistent worry is worth taking seriously early.

School refusal and stomach aches: when worry shows up in the body

A 2025 meta-analysis in the journal Pediatrics pooled 66 studies with 201,134 children aged 4 to 18. It estimated that 11.7%, more than 1 in 9, had a functional abdominal pain disorder, defined by standard criteria called the Rome criteria. The authors list anxiety, stress, negative life events and poor sleep among the contributing factors. These are links, not proof that worry causes the pain.

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) lists stomach aches and headaches among the signs of anxiety. In school avoidance, it says these symptoms appear mostly on school days and are usually absent at weekends. First, it says, ask your child's doctor to check for a physical illness. Then acknowledge your child's worries while insisting on a prompt return, because the longer a child stays home, the harder the return becomes. If avoidance lasts more than 1 week, you may need professional help.

In England, Department for Education (DfE) advice of 7 February 2023 says many children feel normal but difficult emotions about school, such as worries about friendships or schoolwork, and are still expected to attend. It notes that being in school may help with the underlying issue, and that a long absence may heighten anxiety about going back. Schools and parents should agree a plan together.

The NHS suggests asking the school about a meeting with the teacher or the special educational needs coordinator (SENCO), or a quiet space. Ask what the worry is about. It may be a test or another child. If unkindness is behind it, see our friends and bullying guide.

In Hoppo's view, a child who says "I feel sick" when the real problem is a worry is often struggling to name a hard feeling. That is different from deliberate untruths, which our lying guide covers.

What helps: 8 tools from the evidence

  1. Name the feeling, then listen. The NHS advises listening carefully and not dismissing worries. Try "You look worried about tomorrow. Tell me about it."
  2. Explain the wave, then breathe. "Worry is like a wave. It gets big, and then it goes down again." The NHS suggests 3 slow breaths, in for a count of 3 and out for 3.
  3. Take small steps toward the fear. Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) includes gradually facing feared situations, says the AAP. The UK charity Charlie Waller Trust suggests breaking a hard thing into smaller steps. For a party: talk about it, go for 15 minutes with you, then stay longer while you wait nearby. The NHS warns against forcing a child into a stressful situation without first talking about what is making them anxious, so agree each step together.
  4. Answer once, then step out of the reassurance loop. The Charlie Waller Trust says parents can sometimes over-reassure anxious children. Each answer can bring short relief, then the next question. Answer once. Next time, try "We talked about that at dinner. What do you remember I said?" This is Hoppo's suggested wording.
  5. Show confidence, and do less of the avoiding for them. Parents often change what they do to spare a child distress or help them avoid a fear, such as answering for a shy child or changing family routines. Researchers call this accommodation. A 2021 meta-analysis found a moderate link between more accommodation and more severe anxiety when parents reported both, which is a link, not proof. The Charlie Waller Trust suggests saying "I believe you can do it".
  6. Park the worry. The NHS suggests a worry box, made from a tissue box, where children write or draw worries to sort through later. Hoppo also suggests a short, fixed time to open it together.
  7. Keep days predictable, and prepare for change. The NHS advises routines and explaining what will happen, and why, before changes such as a house move. For sleep, see our school-age sleep guide.
  8. Look after your own worry. The NHS advises parents to avoid becoming overprotective or anxious themselves.

Parent-led CBT: when you become the coach

CBT teaches children to notice anxious thoughts and face fears step by step. A 2020 Cochrane review combined 87 studies with 5,964 children and young people with anxiety disorders. After treatment, 49.4% of those given CBT no longer had their main anxiety diagnosis, against 17.8% on a waiting list. About 3 children need CBT for 1 extra recovery. The evidence was rated moderate quality. CBT did not clearly beat usual care.

For younger children, much of the work can go through parents. In a trial at 4 NHS services in Oxfordshire, published in 2017, 136 children aged 5 to 12 referred for anxiety got 1 of 2 brief treatments. After treatment, 59% of children whose parents were guided to deliver CBT were much or very much improved, against 69% after solution-focused brief therapy. The difference was not statistically significant. Parent-led CBT cost about 448 GBP less per child, although that saving was not certain.

A US trial published in 2020 randomised 124 children aged 7 to 14 with anxiety disorders to CBT or to Supportive Parenting for Anxious Childhood Emotions (SPACE). In SPACE only the parents saw the therapist, and the aim was to reduce accommodation. SPACE did as well as CBT on anxiety and reduced accommodation more.

The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) social anxiety guideline of 22 May 2013 recommends 8 to 12 sessions of 45 minutes of individual CBT, or group CBT, for children. It says to consider involving parents, particularly with young children.

The small print. These trials included children referred for or diagnosed with anxiety, so they do not show that every worried child needs therapy. Samples were modest. Medicines are a decision for a specialist.

The short version

  • Fears are common. In 1 study of 190 children aged 4 to 12, 3 in 4 reported fears.
  • Worry may need help when it is out of proportion, lasts, keeps growing or stops your child doing ordinary things.
  • Repeated tummy aches on school days can be a sign of worry. See your general practitioner (GP) first, then plan a quick return with the school.
  • Name the feeling, take small steps toward the fear, answer once and step out of reassurance loops.
  • In trials of children referred for anxiety, parent-led treatments did about as well as other therapies.

When to ask your doctor, school nurse or school for help

  • Anxiety is severe, does not go away or stops your child doing things they enjoy. The NHS suggests starting with your GP or school nurse, and talking to the school if school life is affected.
  • Your child has avoided school for more than 1 week (AAP), or has regular tummy aches or headaches on school days. Ask your GP to check for illness, and meet the teacher or SENCO.
  • Your child seems constantly distressed, has ongoing sleep or eating problems, many negative thoughts or is avoiding friends (NHS, Healthdirect).
  • You are not sure how to help. In the UK, the YoungMinds parents helpline is free on 0808 802 5544 on weekdays from 9.30am (NHS, YoungMinds). Check the YoungMinds website for current opening times.
  • Same day: if your child needs urgent help for their mental health, call NHS 111 and choose the mental health option, or ask for an urgent GP appointment. Call 999 or go to accident and emergency (A&E) if a life is at risk or you cannot keep your child safe (NHS). Across Europe, call 112.
  • In the UK, Childline is free on 0800 1111 for under-19s (NHS). In Slovenia, the TOM helpline is free on 116 111, every day from midday to 8pm.

From 1 child to 2: how to prepare your toddler for a new baby, and the 10 minutes a day that can help

You love your toddler fiercely, and you are about to hand them the biggest change of their short life. You may also be quietly wondering how you will feed a newborn while a 2-year-old climbs the bookcase. Here is how to prepare, which setbacks are normal, and the small habits that make the first months easier.

Does it get easier? A question many parents ask

In April 2026, a Mumsnet parent of a 6-month-old and a nearly 3-year-old simply asked whether life with a toddler and a baby ever gets easier. A National Health Service (NHS) maternity leaflet from Dartford and Gravesham is frank: coping with 2 children is very different from coping with 1.

The good news comes with a number. The Australian parenting site Raising Children Network says almost every child needs time to adjust, and a positive sibling relationship usually develops by the time the baby is about 14 months old. The hard part is a phase.

Before the birth: simple words, baby books and a real baby to look at

  • Tell them in toddler time. Raising Children Network suggests talking about the baby at least 3 to 4 months before the birth, linked to something they know, such as "after Grandma's birthday".
  • Read books about babies and big brothers and sisters. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) says this helps toddlers learn words like "sister", "brother" and "new baby".
  • Be honest. The AAP suggests explaining that the baby will cry, need a lot of attention, and that everyone must be gentle.
  • Visit a real newborn. Raising Children Network says time with friends' babies shows how sleepy and needy newborns are. A toddler expecting a playmate may otherwise be disappointed.
  • Practise with a doll. Just One Norfolk, an NHS family service, suggests a doll to show how to hold a baby, protecting the head and neck.
  • Explain who will look after them during the birth, as Ireland's Health Service Executive (HSE) suggests.

Moving bed or potty training? The timing that health services agree on

The advice is consistent across countries. Make big changes well before the birth, or wait until the baby is settled.

The first meeting: free arms and a big hug

  • Free your arms. Just One Norfolk suggests having the baby in a cot, Moses basket or someone else's arms when your toddler comes in, so you can give your toddler full attention.
  • Hug first. Raising Children Network says a big, loving hug before the introduction is especially important for toddlers.
  • A small gift "from the baby" is a tradition both Raising Children Network and Mater mention.
  • Let them touch gently, if they want to. No interest is fine too. Children come to it in their own time (Just One Norfolk).
  • Ask visitors to greet your toddler first (Pregnancy, Birth and Baby, an Australian Government service). The AAP also suggests visitors spend time with your older child.

Wanting a bottle or nappies again is normal, and it passes

Many toddlers go backwards for a while. They may ask for a bottle or dummy, want to be carried, wet themselves again or wake at night. The AAP, HSE, NHS 111 Wales and Raising Children Network all call this normal, and common during the baby's first year.

  • Stay calm. NHS 111 Wales advises patience and no punishment.
  • Skip "act your age". The AAP suggests praising grown-up behaviour instead.
  • If your toddler asks to breastfeed, Raising Children Network says there is no right or wrong answer. You can offer a breastfeed if you are comfortable, a taste of breastmilk from a cup, or a special drink instead.

HealthLink BC, a Canadian health service, puts it simply: this will not last long.

Jealousy is expected, and even 10 minutes alone can help

NHS 111 Wales tells parents to expect some jealousy. The National Childbirth Trust (NCT), a UK parent charity, calls it a natural reaction to the fear of being replaced. Some toddlers are angry with the baby, others suddenly prefer 1 parent, which HSE also lists as normal.

The most repeated advice is daily 1-to-1 time. HSE says even 10 minutes alone with your older child is good.

  • Make it special time just for them, while the baby sleeps (Nemours KidsHealth).
  • If you have a partner, take turns: 1 adult has the baby, the other has the toddler.
  • Keep their rituals, such as the same bedtime song (Raising Children Network).
  • Praise your toddler in front of visitors (HSE).

Safety with a toddler near a newborn: 4 rules that matter

Toddlers love hard, and they cannot yet judge their own strength. The NCT notes that a jealous older child may physically hurt the baby.

  1. Do not leave them alone together. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby, an Australian Government health service, says never leave a toddler alone with a baby. A toddler may share food that could choke the baby, or rock the pram until it tips over. Raising Children Network suggests letting your toddler touch the baby when you are there to supervise. We suggest taking the baby with you, even to answer the door.
  2. Holding happens sitting down, with you. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby suggests your toddler holds the baby only with your help, for example sitting on your lap. The AAP adds that older children should ask first, and you should teach them how. Or sit your toddler on the sofa with cushions, lay the baby across their lap and keep your hand under the baby's head.
  3. Use the toilet roll test. The Canadian Paediatric Society says that if an object fits inside a toilet paper roll, it is a choking hazard. A children's emergency nurse, writing for the Institute of Health Visiting, says that in her experience younger siblings seem to have more choking incidents than first-borns, often involving an older child's small toys. Keep small pieces where the baby cannot reach, especially once she crawls.
  4. The baby only has milk. The NHS advises solid food from around 6 months. Teach a simple rule: no snacks or toys in the baby's mouth.

The feeding basket: how to feed the baby while your toddler is busy

Feeds are when many toddlers play up, because your arms are taken. Raising Children Network suggests a special box of toys kept only for feed times. HealthLink BC and Mater add a snack, music or a story. A feeding basket might hold:

  • 2 or 3 board books, so you can read aloud while you feed
  • A sticker book or magnetic drawing board
  • A doll or soft toy with a toy bottle, so your toddler can "feed" too
  • A snack and a drink in a lidded cup
  • An audio story, and 1 rotated toy each week to keep it exciting

Keep the basket out of sight between feeds, and set it by your chair before you sit down.

Sleep with 2 children: protect the toddler bedtime first

Raising Children Network lists refusing bedtime and night waking among common setbacks after a baby arrives.

  • Keep the toddler routine. Same bath, book and song, even with the baby in a sling.
  • Divide the evening. If you have a partner, 1 of you does the toddler's bedtime while the other feeds the baby.
  • Keep the baby's sleep space safe. The Lullaby Trust, a UK safer sleep charity, says babies should sleep on their back in their own cot or Moses basket, free from toys, blankets and pillows, so teach your toddler not to put toys in it. If you co-sleep, do not bring other children or pets into bed. See our safe sleep guide.

Your own tiredness counts too

The NHS advises resting when your baby sleeps and choosing rest over housework. With a toddler awake, that nap often disappears, so plan for it.

  • Ask for help with the toddler, too. The NHS suggests a relative minds the baby while you nap. A grandparent taking your toddler to the park works just as well.
  • Share the nights if you can. The NHS suggests a partner helps with nappies or dressing in the morning so you can go back to sleep.
  • Get outside once a day. The NHS says regular exercise can help you feel less tired.

"Will I love the second as much?" A worry with a reassuring answer

Many parents think this during a second pregnancy, then feel guilty for thinking it. The research is mostly reassuring.

In a US study of about 240 mothers expecting a second baby, reported by the University of Michigan in June 2023, about 70% did not worry at all about bonding with the new baby. The families were followed until the babies were 12 months old. Worry in pregnancy did not predict how securely the babies were attached to their mothers at 1 year, although mothers who worried more showed less warmth towards their babies in the first 8 months.

Bonding does not have to happen in the first minute. Tommy's, the UK pregnancy charity, says some parents bond straight away while others find their love grows slowly over the first few weeks, and both are completely normal. In the same study, strong worry often came with low mood, so if it fills your days, tell your midwife, GP or health visitor.

The short version

  • Prepare early with simple words, books and a real baby to meet. Make big changes well before the birth or well after.
  • Bottles, accidents and baby talk are normal setbacks, and they pass.
  • Even 10 minutes of 1-to-1 time a day can help with jealousy.
  • Never leave a toddler alone with the baby, and keep anything that fits in a toilet roll away from her.
  • A good sibling bond usually forms by about 14 months.

When to talk to your doctor or health visitor

  • You cannot sleep even when the baby sleeps, feel down or hopeless, or stop enjoying things. The NHS lists these as possible signs of postnatal depression and advises talking to a GP or health visitor as soon as possible.
  • You have thoughts of harming yourself or your baby. The NHS lists this as a symptom of postnatal depression. Get help urgently: in the UK, call 111 and choose the mental health option, or call 999 in an emergency.
  • Your toddler's setbacks or aggression do not ease with time. HealthLink BC and HSE suggest your public health nurse or GP.
  • Your toddler or baby is choking and cannot breathe, cough or cry. Call your emergency number (999 in the UK, 112 across Europe, 911 in the US) and follow our choking guide.

Gentle or firm? 60 years of research favours parents who are both

Your feed tells you to validate every feeling. Your mother tells you children today get away with everything. You are left wondering which camp you belong to, and whether you are getting it wrong. Here is what 6 decades of research actually found, why smacking is the 1 thing experts agree to drop, and 7 everyday tools that work from age 1 to 5.

Searches for "positive discipline" are up 295%. Parents are tired of picking a team

Pinterest's Parenting Trend Report 2026, published on 24 February 2026, says searches for "positive discipline" rose 295% and "authoritative parenting" rose 115%. Treat these as a signal of interest, not a study. Pinterest gives no time period, method, region or base numbers for these figures.

At the same time, gentle parenting is losing some fans. The US parenting site The Bump put "hybrid" parenting first in its list of trends for 2026, and reported that fewer than 40% of Gen Z parents say they use gentle parenting. That figure comes from an online survey of 2,000 US parents of children aged 0 to 6, run by Talker Research for the childcare chain Kiddie Academy in August 2025, which found 32%. It is a commercial poll, so read it with care. A psychotherapist quoted in the same piece put the problem simply: somewhere along the way, gentle got confused with permissive.

The worry behind all this is very human. In October 2026, parents on Mumsnet's behaviour board asked whether their almost 3 year old's behaviour was normal, how to help a 4 year old with big feelings, and what to do when a child is not bothered at all by being told off.

The 1966 paper that named a third way

In December 1966, the US psychologist Diana Baumrind published a paper in the journal Child Development that still shapes the debate. She described 3 broad styles. Later researchers added a fourth.

StyleWarmthClear limitsWhat it sounds like
AuthoritarianLowHigh"Because I said so."
PermissiveHighLow"OK, 1 more biscuit, just do not cry."
AuthoritativeHighHigh"I know you are cross. Biscuits are finished. Let us find your cup."
NeglectfulLowLowLittle response either way.

The authoritative style is warm and firm at the same time. It is the style behind that 115% rise in Pinterest searches. It is also, in plain words, what most parents mean when they say they want to be "kind but not a pushover".

What 1,435 studies say, with the honest small print

In 2017 the psychologist Martin Pinquart combined 1,435 studies on parenting and children's behaviour problems, such as aggression and rule-breaking. Warmth, clear rules and an authoritative style were linked with fewer problems. Harsh control, authoritarian, permissive and neglectful styles were linked with more. The strongest links of all were for harsh control and for psychological control, such as guilt-tripping or withdrawing love.

A second review in 2018, of 428 studies, asked whether this only holds in Western families. In every world region, the authoritative style was linked with at least 1 good outcome for children, with some regional variation.

3 honest limits. The links for warmth, clear rules and the authoritative style were "very small to small", so parenting style is 1 influence among many, alongside temperament, sleep, health and family stress. Most studies observe families and cannot prove cause. The links also run both ways: a child's behaviour shapes how parents respond, as well as the other way round.

So if your child has a fierce temper despite your best efforts, that is not proof you chose the wrong style.

Smacking: the 1 point where experts and 70 countries agree

On physical punishment, the evidence is unusually clear. A 2016 analysis by Elizabeth Gershoff and Andrew Grogan-Kaylor pooled data on 160,927 children. Smacking, also called spanking, was linked with worse results in 13 of 17 outcomes, including more aggression and antisocial behaviour. None was linked with better behaviour.

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) said in its 2018 policy statement that all forms of physical punishment, and yelling at or shaming children, work little in the short term and not at all in the long term.

The World Health Organization (WHO) updated its fact sheet on 11 May 2026. It estimates that 1.2 billion children aged 0 to 18 are physically punished at home each year. It says physical punishment has no positive outcomes and increases behaviour problems over time. A 2025 WHO report found that children who were physically punished were 24% less likely to be developmentally on track, based on data from 49 low- and middle-income countries.

The law has followed. Sweden was the first country to ban all physical punishment of children, in 1979. The End Corporal Punishment initiative now counts 70 countries with a full ban, including Czech Republic and Switzerland in 2025. Even so, it says only 15% of the world's children are fully protected by law.

What gentle parenting gets right, and where it gets misread

There is very little research on "gentle parenting" under that name. It is a social media label rather than a defined programme. But its core ideas line up well with the evidence.

  • It gets right: warmth, no hitting or shaming, and looking for the feeling behind the behaviour. The United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF) gives the same advice for ages 0 to 5.
  • It gets misread: when "gentle" turns into "no limits", it slides into the permissive style, which the reviews above link with more behaviour problems, not fewer.

The "firm" camp has a point too. A 2025 analysis pooled 14 European trials of parenting programmes, with 3,252 families of children aged 1 to 13. The 2 changes that best explained calmer behaviour were following through on what parents said, and less harsh telling-off, such as shouting. Being calm and being consistent turned out to be partners.

So Hoppo's suggestion is simple. You do not have to pick a side. Take the warmth from gentle parenting and the follow-through from firm parenting, and drop the harshness from both.

7 tools that work from age 1 to 5

  1. Connect, then correct. Get down to their eye level and name the feeling before the limit: "You are sad the park is finished. We are going home now." UNICEF notes that 10 minutes of real one-to-one time a day can make a big difference. Hoppo's weekly 5-minute practice skill is an easy way to build that time in.
  2. Keep rules few and clear. 3 rules a toddler can remember beat 20 they cannot. Say what to do, not only what to stop: "Feet on the floor" instead of "Stop climbing". UNICEF stresses clear, age-appropriate limits, repeated the same way every time. A steady daily rhythm helps too, as our toddler routine chart shows.
  3. Let consequences be natural and short. Sand thrown at the park means you leave the sandpit now, not "no television for a week". The AAP advises explaining the consequence calmly and following through straight away. Under about 2, simply move your child or the object, and offer something they can do.
  4. Use a time-in or a short calm-down. Experts disagree here. A 2019 review in American Psychologist found strong evidence for brief, calm time-out used within a warm relationship. Critics worry that sending a small child away feels like rejection. A time-in means you stay close while they settle. Hoppo suggests staying near under-2s, and for older toddlers choosing whichever version you can do calmly. The AAP's rule of thumb is about 1 minute per year of age. Afterwards, reconnect with a hug.
  5. Praise the effort, not the child. "You kept trying with that zip" works better than "Clever girl". A small 2013 study of 53 children found that parents' effort praise at 14 to 38 months predicted, 5 years later, a stronger belief that ability grows with practice. It observed families, so it shows a link rather than proof.
  6. Stay calm, or take your own pause. The AAP says you can give yourself a time-out if you feel out of control, once your child is safe. UNICEF suggests 10 slow, deep breaths before reacting.
  7. Repair after you lose your temper. Every parent shouts sometimes. When you are calm, keep it short: "I shouted. That was not OK. I am sorry. I love you." Saying sorry shows your child the very skill you are asking them to learn.

The short version

  • You do not have to choose between gentle and firm. 60 years of research favours warm parenting with clear limits.
  • Smacking, shouting and shaming do not improve behaviour over time. 70 countries now ban physical punishment.
  • Gentle parenting works when it keeps the limits. Without limits it becomes permissive.
  • Few rules, calm follow-through, short consequences and a quick repair after a bad moment do most of the work.
  • Style is 1 influence among many. A spirited toddler is not proof you are doing it wrong.

When to ask your doctor or health visitor for help

  • Your child's behaviour worries you, or is getting worse over several weeks rather than better.
  • Your child often hurts other children or adults, or nursery or childminder staff raise concerns.
  • Behaviour problems come with worries about speech, sleep, eating or development, or your child loses words or skills they used to have.
  • You feel stressed, angry or low most days, or you are shouting more than you want to. Ask about a parenting programme. The WHO recommends them in its 2023 guidelines, and they are designed to help, not judge.
  • If you ever feel you might hurt your child, put them somewhere safe, step away and call someone you trust. Talk to your doctor or health visitor the same day. If a child is in danger, call your local emergency number.

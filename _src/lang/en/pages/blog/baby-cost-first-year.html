What a baby really costs in the first year, and what you can get back

You see a headline saying a baby costs £5,000 in the first year, and you quietly wonder how anyone manages. Many parents on Mumsnet say those numbers look nothing like what they actually spent. Here are real, dated ranges for Europe and the UK, the 2 costs that matter far more than the pram, and the money most families can claim back.

Our estimate: about 1,500 to 6,500 EUR, and you choose where you land

The lowest credible UK figure comes from MoneyHelper, the free guidance service backed by the UK government. Its "very basic" first year costs about £1,645. That is nearly £600 in month 1, then about £95 a month (updated 20 July 2026, based on product prices from idealo).

The highest figures assume you buy everything new and use formula. A study by the money transfer company Remitly, reported by Euronews on 20 September 2026, priced 14 typical baby products in 38 European countries in July 2026. It put the European average at 4,727 EUR for the first year. The UK was cheapest at 2,813 EUR and Spain most expensive at 6,294 EUR. Germany came to 4,701 EUR. Childcare was not included.

In Germany, the savings bank group Sparkasse puts a full set of new first equipment at about 1,640 to 4,580 EUR (updated 22 September 2026).

Put together, Hoppo estimates roughly 1,500 to 3,000 EUR in baby things if you breastfeed, buy some kit used and skip the extras. Expect 4,500 to 6,500 EUR if you buy everything new and use formula. This is our reading of the sources above, not a published figure.

Why the big averages feel wrong to so many parents

In 2011, a widely reported survey said the first year cost £5,200. On Mumsnet, one parent pointed out that it came from a company selling baby equipment. A parent who had spent about £900 asked who could have spent enough to push the average above £5,000. Totals shared on the first pages of that thread ran from about £900 to over £5,000. For one parent, nursery fees made up more than half of a £4,000 total.

In a small 2011 poll on the MoneySavingExpert forum, 50 of 61 voters (82%) said they spent under £100 a month on baby items.

Even careful studies make big assumptions. In the Remitly study, clothes were the largest category, at an average of 1,465 EUR across Europe, ahead of formula (1,187 EUR) and nappies (1,152 EUR). Families with gifts and hand-me-downs often spend far less.

The 2 costs that catch people out are not sold in any shop

When someone planning their first baby asked on the MoneySavingExpert forum what a baby really costs, experienced parents agreed. The real money goes on the drop in salary during leave, and on childcare afterwards.

Lost income. In the UK, Statutory Maternity Pay is 90% of your average weekly earnings for 6 weeks, then £194.32 a week, or 90% if that is lower, for 33 more weeks (GOV.UK, 2026 to 2027 rate). A simple example, before tax and without employer top-ups: if you earn £500 a week, you would receive about £9,100 over those 39 weeks instead of £19,500. That is a gap of about £10,400, more than most families spend on baby things all year. Some countries replace more of your pay. In Germany, parental allowance (Elterngeld) replaces 65% of previous net income for higher earners and up to 100% for lower earners, between 300 and 1,800 EUR a month (Federal Ministry for Family Affairs, BMFSFJ).

Childcare. If you return to work in year 1, childcare can cost more than everything else combined. In the UK, part-time nursery for a child under 2 costs about £8,194 a year. In England, eligible working parents can now get up to 30 hours of free childcare a week, for 38 weeks a year, from 9 months (GOV.UK). Rules differ in Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland and every other country, so check yours before you plan your return.

What parents most often say they wasted money on

In a long Mumsnet thread from 2020 about what first-time parents do not need, these came up again and again:

  • A special nappy disposal bin
  • A baby bath, when the sink or a normal bath worked fine
  • A changing unit, when a portable mat moved from room to room
  • A playpen that ended up storing toys
  • A bottle warmer
  • A large, bulky travel system
  • Lots of 0 to 3 month clothes, tiny shoes and fancy outfits
  • Too many toys

The same parents would happily buy these again: plenty of muslin cloths, a safe sleep space, a good car seat and a bouncer. Some loved a white noise app, and formula feeders praised a bottle preparation machine.

The 4 savings that make the biggest difference

  1. Buy the right things second hand. Which? (31 October 2025) says prams, most cots and clothes are generally fine to buy used, if you check safety labels and recalls. A cot mattress is different. Which? cites Lullaby Trust research that found a higher risk of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) with a second-hand mattress from outside the family home. Our safe sleep guide explains what to look for.
  2. Say yes to hand-me-downs and practical gifts. Forum parents say these cut their totals the most. If people ask, MoneyHelper suggests nappies or vouchers towards a big item.
  3. Choose own-brand nappies and cheaper formula. The National Health Service (NHS) says all formula brands meet a baby's nutritional needs, whatever the price. The CMA found that many parents choose a brand while vulnerable, often in hospital just after birth. Choosing a low-priced brand can save about £300 to £500 in year 1 if you formula feed from birth (CMA).
  4. Wait until your baby arrives. Many regretted buys depend on your baby's size, temperament and feeding, which nobody can know in advance. Our newborn shopping list shows what to get now and what can wait. The growth log in Hoppo shows when your baby is nearing the next clothes size, so you buy it only when needed.

Car seats: the buy where cheaper is fine but second hand is not

The Royal Society for the Prevention of Accidents (RoSPA) is clear: "The best advice is do not buy a second-hand child seat." A seat may have hidden damage from a crash or a drop, missing instructions, or be too old for current standards. If you accept one, RoSPA says it should come from family or friends, with a full history you are sure of, the original instructions and an age within the maker's limit.

New does not have to mean expensive. MoneyHelper says cheaper car seats pass the same safety tests as premium ones. On a new seat, look for the approval label with an E in a circle and R129, the newer standard that includes i-Size seats.

What you can get back: most European countries help more than parents expect

Most European countries support new parents with some mix of a birth grant paid once, a monthly child benefit and paid parental leave. Amounts, rules and deadlines vary widely. Here are 3 examples.

United Kingdom (2026 to 2027 rates). Child Benefit is £27.05 a week for your first or only child, although higher earners may have to pay some or all of it back. That is about £1,407 a year by our arithmetic, close to the MoneyHelper "very basic" year of £1,645. Families on certain benefits can claim a £500 Sure Start Maternity Grant for a first child, from 11 weeks before the due date until 6 months after the birth. It is not available in Scotland, which has a Pregnancy and Baby Payment instead.

Germany (2026). Child benefit (Kindergeld) is 259 EUR a month per child from January 2026, according to the Federal Employment Agency. Parents can share 14 months of Elterngeld, with each taking 2 to 12 months.

Finland (2026). The social insurance institution Kela offers a maternity grant: a box of baby clothes and care products, or 210 EUR tax-free cash. You need a medical check before the end of month 4 of pregnancy, and must apply at least 2 months before the due date.

Check your own country. Search your government website for "birth grant", "child benefit" and "parental leave", and ask your midwife or employer. Note every deadline, because some grants must be claimed before the birth. Many cities and regions add their own payments too.

The first year in 5 lines

  • Baby things usually cost about 1,500 to 3,000 EUR on a lean budget and 4,500 to 6,500 EUR all new with formula (Hoppo estimate).
  • The biggest costs are lost income during leave and childcare, not the pram.
  • Buy prams, cots and clothes second hand if you like. Buy the car seat and cot mattress new, and wait for the baby before buying extras.
  • All formula brands meet nutritional needs. A cheaper one can save about £300 to £500 in year 1 if you formula feed from birth.
  • Claim your birth grant, child benefit and parental pay, and note the deadlines.

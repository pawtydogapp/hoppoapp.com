Who remembers the vaccinations? A fair way to share the invisible work after a baby, in 15 minutes a week

You are the one who knows the nappies run out on Thursday, the 8-week jabs are next Tuesday and the vests no longer fit. Your partner is kind and willing, and still asks you where the wipes are. Here is what research says about how the work really splits after a baby, why it matters, and simple ways to share it before it turns into a row.

119 replies to 1 mother: you are far from the only one

In September 2026, a Mum on Mumsnet wrote that her baby was 6 weeks old, her partner was not helping and she did not know what to do. The thread drew 119 replies and became one of the busiest on the childbirth board.

Many replies described the same pattern. The partner does jobs when asked. The Mum does the asking, planning and remembering, and that part is often the most tiring.

If this sounds like your home, it is very common, there are clear reasons for it, and it can change.

The mental load: the work that happens before anyone lifts a finger

The mental load is the thinking work of running a family. It is the noticing, planning and remembering that comes before the doing.

Sociologist Allison Daminger interviewed 35 US couples for a study in the American Sociological Review in 2019. She split this thinking work into 4 steps:

  1. Anticipate. Notice a need is coming. The baby will grow out of size 2 nappies soon.
  2. Identify options. Which size, which shop, how many.
  3. Decide. Choose 1 option.
  4. Monitor. Check it happened, and notice when the need comes back.

Daminger found that women did more of this work overall, especially the anticipating and the monitoring. Deciding was shared fairly evenly. The thinking work was often invisible, even to the person doing it, and a frequent cause of conflict.

So 1 parent buys the nappies when asked. The other noticed they were low, worked out the size and wrote the list. Both feel they did the job.

What really changes after a first baby: the numbers

Research suggests the split often drifts after the first birth, even in couples who shared well before.

StudyWhere and whenWhat it found

Same study, houseworkBefore birth and at 9 monthsEach partner did about 15 hours of housework a week before. Afterwards, men's fell to about 10. Women's stayed at about 15, on top of baby care.

There is good news too. In a poll quoted by The Bump in December 2025, about half of Gen Z parents said they split work and home duties evenly. The poll's method was not published, so treat it as a sign of changing hopes.

The drift rarely comes from bad intentions. Often 1 parent takes longer leave, or breastfeeds, and becomes the expert simply by being there more. After a few months, the gap feels natural, and nobody chose it.

Why fairness matters for both of you, and for the baby

Relationships often dip after a baby. A US study, published in 2009, followed 218 couples over their first 8 years of marriage. Couples who had a baby showed a sudden drop in how well the relationship worked after the birth. Couples without children declined more slowly.

A second US study, published in 2011 with national survey data on 569 wives, looked at why new mothers became less happy in their marriage. Their drop in happiness was linked to less time alone with their partner and a stronger feeling that housework was unfair. The split itself mattered less than whether it felt fair.

Mental health is part of this. NHS England says up to 20% of women have a mental health problem during pregnancy or in the first year after birth. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that 13% of women who have just given birth have a mental disorder, mostly depression.

Partners are affected too. The NHS says fathers and partners can also have depression after a baby. A 2010 review in the Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) pooled 43 studies and found depression in about 10% of expectant and new fathers, and it was linked to depression in the mother. When 1 parent struggles, the other often does too.

You may also have seen the word matrescence, the identity shift of becoming a mother. Searches for it rose by 5,000% in early 2026, during an advertising campaign by the parenting app Peanut, according to Chief Marketer. The spike came with a marketing push, but the feeling is real for many parents, and a fair load leaves more room to adjust.

Owning beats helping: hand over whole tasks, end to end

"Just tell me what to do" sounds kind. It still leaves 1 parent as the manager. The fix is to hand over whole tasks, including the thinking steps, so 1 person owns each job from noticing to finishing.

TaskHelping looks likeOwning looks like
Vaccinations and check-upsGoing to the appointment when remindedKnowing the schedule, booking, going, and putting the next date in the calendar
Nappies and wipesBuying them when askedChecking stock, moving up a size in time, never running out
Baby clothesDressing the baby in what is laid outNoticing what is too small, sorting it, and getting the next size
Bath and bedtimeDoing bathtime on requestRunning the whole routine every night, including the towel wash

The owner does the job their way. If the vest goes on back to front, let it go. Constant corrections hand the job straight back.

The 15-minute Sunday check-in that can head off rows

Pick a fixed time each week, when the baby is asleep and neither of you is starving.

  1. Look at the week ahead. Appointments, visitors, work trips.
  2. Name what is coming. Jabs due, nappy size change, nursery forms, a birthday present to buy.
  3. Give each job 1 owner. Write their name next to it.
  4. Plan rest. When does each of you get time off this week?
  5. Ask 1 question. "Is anything feeling unfair or heavy right now?" Listen, then fix 1 thing.

Nights, shifts and protected rest

Split-shift parenting was named as a trend for 2026 by The Bump. Each parent takes a turn with the baby while the other rests or does something else. With babies, it often means splitting the night.

  • Formula feeding: the NHS suggests partners share the feeds. You could split the night, for example 9 pm to 2 am for 1 parent and 2 am to 7 am for the other.
  • Breastfeeding: the NHS suggests a partner changes nappies or dresses the baby in the morning, so Mum can go back to sleep. Once breastfeeding is established, a partner can sometimes give expressed milk at night.
  • Protected rest: each parent gets at least 1 block a week that is truly off, out of the house or behind a closed door.

The NHS also advises resting when the baby sleeps, because rest may matter more than chores. Our baby sleep guide explains what nights look like by age.

How to raise it without starting a fight

The NHS puts it simply: your partner cannot read your mind. The charity Tommy's warns that keeping feelings to yourself can cause bigger problems later.

  • Choose a calm moment. Not at 3 am, and not mid-argument about the dishwasher.
  • Start with how you feel. "I feel like I am carrying the whole list in my head, and I am exhausted."
  • Show the work. Write down everything you track for 1 week. Many partners never saw it.
  • Ask for ownership. "Could you take over the appointments, start to finish?" works better than "You never help."
  • Expect a learning curve. The first month of owning a job is slower.

Partners who want to do more can simply pick 1 task and take it over fully.

Same-sex couples, co-parents and single parents

These ideas work in any family. In 2-Mum or 2-Dad homes, the parent on longer leave can become the default expert in just the same way. Parents who live apart can agree clear roles and check in regularly, as Tommy's suggests.

If you are parenting alone, your support network can still take whole tasks. Ask 1 friend to own the weekly shop, or a relative to take the baby for a nap slot each week. The NHS suggests asking friends or family to stay for a few days if you are on your own.

The short version

  • The mental load is noticing, planning and remembering, as well as doing.
  • After a first baby, the work often drifts towards 1 parent, usually without anyone choosing it.
  • A sense of unfairness, more than the exact split, is linked to unhappiness in the relationship.
  • Depression after a birth affects mothers and partners. A fairer load may help protect both of you.
  • Own whole tasks, hold a 15-minute weekly check-in, share 1 calendar, and give each parent protected rest.

When to talk to your doctor or health visitor

  • Low mood, hopelessness or feeling you cannot cope lasts more than 2 weeks. The NHS says the baby blues usually pass within 2 weeks of the birth. This applies to partners as well as mothers.
  • You have trouble sleeping even when you get the chance, or feel guilty or worthless as a parent (NHS).
  • You have thoughts of harming yourself or your baby. Get help the same day from your GP. If you or your baby are in danger now, call your local emergency number.
  • Conflict at home feels frightening or controlling, or includes threats or violence. Talk to your GP, midwife or health visitor, or a domestic abuse support line in your country. If you are in danger now, call your local emergency number. The NHS says nobody has to put up with domestic abuse.

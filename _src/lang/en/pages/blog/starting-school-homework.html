Starting school without battles: the 10-minute homework rule and what is normal in the first term

Your child held it together all day, then fell apart over the wrong colour cup. The reading book is still in the bag. You are wondering whether this is normal, and how much homework is too much. Here is what the first term usually looks like, what research says about homework in primary school, and the small routines that keep evenings calm.

The first term is tiring for almost everyone

Parents have described the same pattern for over a decade. In a Mumsnet thread from November 2012, a parent wrote that her daughter, new to full-time Reception, was so tired she often cried and had tantrums. Many replies blamed tiredness, and said things eased after a few weeks or after Christmas. These are parent reports, not research.

Older children feel it too. On 15 September 2026, a Mumsnet parent asked whether it was normal that her nearly 8 year old had been screaming and snapping for 2 weeks since the summer holidays, while school itself seemed to be going well.

Sleep is part of the picture. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) recommends 10 to 13 hours in 24 hours for children aged 3 to 5, including naps, and 9 to 12 hours for children aged 6 to 12. A school counsellor quoted by the Slovenian news site N1 in August 2025 suggested bedtime by 20:00 for first graders. See our school-age sleep guide.

Restraint collapse, clinginess and Monday tummy aches

"After-school restraint collapse" is a parenting term, not a diagnosis. It describes children who manage well at school, then cry, shout or turn silly at home. Psychologist Robyn Koslowitz, writing for Psychology Today on 5 September 2025, called it "not pathological". A day of rules and self-control uses up energy. Home is where children feel safe enough to let go.

Henry Ford Health, a US hospital system, said on 9 October 2026 that the pattern is normal. The 2 sources suggest:

  • Feed first, ask later. Water, a snack and a toilet trip come before questions.
  • Leave a buffer. Henry Ford suggests about 30 to 60 minutes of unstructured time before homework or chores.
  • Move. A walk or a scooter ride home helps a child reset.
  • Delay "How was school?" Try at dinner or bedtime instead: "Tell me 1 good bit and 1 tricky bit of today."
  • Keep the limit, delay the talk. Stop anything unsafe. Discuss the feeling once everyone is calm.

Some children cling at the classroom door for weeks. The National Health Service (NHS) says separation anxiety is very common from about 6 months to 3 years, and that a new school can trigger anxiety in some children. It suggests listening without brushing worries off and keeping routines steady. Keep goodbyes short and the same every day: a hug, a phrase, then leave.

Headaches or stomach aches that come on school days and vanish at weekends can be a sign of school avoidance, says the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) parent site HealthyChildren.org. It advises a check-up, then a calm, firm return, because the longer a child stays home, the harder going back becomes. Our worries guide has more ideas. For limits at home, see gentle or firm.

3 school readiness myths

Myth 1: they should read and write before they start. Slovenia sets no required list of skills for first grade. A school counsellor quoted by N1 said children do not need to write or hold a pencil correctly before school, and that bad habits from early pushing are harder to fix.

Myth 2: holding a child back a year always helps. In Slovenia, postponed school entry rose from 5.3% in 2011/12 to 11.6% in 2021/22. The National Education Institute Slovenia (Zavod Republike Slovenije za šolstvo) wrote in 2024 guidelines that postponement does not automatically help development. In England, the Institute for Fiscal Studies (IFS) found August-born children score lower mostly because they sit national tests about 11 months younger than September-born classmates. A delay can still be right for some children, after a professional assessment.

Myth 3: readiness is about academic skills. A Kindred Squared survey of 1,000 primary school staff and 1,000 parents, reported on 1 September 2026, found staff believed 37% of Reception children were not school ready and 26% were not toilet trained. 88% of parents said their child was ready. It is a survey of opinions, so treat it as a signal.

Opinion from Hoppo: the most useful preparation is dull. Using the toilet alone, washing hands, putting on a coat, opening a lunchbox, saying "I need help". Our starting school guide has a checklist.

How much homework is reasonable, and what the evidence says

The best-known review is by Harris Cooper and colleagues at Duke University, published in 2006. It covered US research from 1987 to 2003 and found generally consistent evidence that homework helps achievement overall. In studies of simple correlations, the link was stronger in grades 7 to 12 than in kindergarten to grade 6. The American Psychological Association's Monitor summed up the primary result in March 2016 as "only faint evidence" of benefit.

  • Quality beats quantity. A 2017 review by Fan and colleagues pooled 28 studies of maths and science homework, with more than 312,000 pupils, mostly in secondary school. The overall link was small (r = 0.23). How well pupils did homework was linked with achievement more strongly (r = 0.53) than time spent (r = 0.19). It found a stronger primary link than Cooper did, so the evidence is not settled.
  • Less help, more support. A 2024 review by Xu and colleagues pooled 28 studies with 378,222 participants. In primary school, more parental homework involvement was weakly linked with lower achievement (r = -0.142). Supporting independence was the only type linked with higher achievement (r = 0.164). These are links only. Children who struggle may simply get more help.

The Education Endowment Foundation (EEF), an English education charity, rates homework at about 3 months of extra progress a year in primary and 5 in secondary, from 43 studies. It rates the evidence as low. It says task quality appears to matter more than amount, and impact appears to fall as time rises.

Cooper backs the 10-minute rule: about 10 minutes a night per school grade. The US National Parent Teacher Association (PTA) endorses it. It is a guide, not a study finding.

Reading together: the homework that matters most early on

The National Literacy Trust surveyed 114,970 children and young people aged 5 to 18 in early 2025. Only 44.5% of 5 to 8 year olds read daily in their free time, down 9.1 percentage points since 2019. Among 8 to 18 year olds, 32.7% enjoyed reading in their free time, a fall of 36% since 2005.

The EEF rates parental engagement at about 4 months of extra progress, from 97 studies, most of them on home reading. It says programmes that involve parents in homework have generally not been linked with higher attainment. A stuck child may be better served by a word with the teacher than by a parent teaching at the kitchen table.

Carleton University's child language lab in Canada summarises a meta-analysis of 20 studies with 1,583 families. Training parents to teach specific reading skills with short exercises had the largest effect. Reading books together also helped, less strongly.

  1. Keep it short. We suggest about 10 minutes with a school book, then stop while it is still fun.
  2. Let them sound it out. Wait a few seconds, then point to the first sound: "What does this letter say?"
  3. Praise the work. "You went back and fixed that word" says more than "Clever boy".
  4. Read to them too. Books above their own level keep the story alive while decoding is slow.
  5. Talk about it. "What do you think happens next?" builds understanding.

A calm routine, and when to talk to the teacher

The AAP's back-to-school advice, updated on 26 August 2026, suggests a regular homework time, a quiet place without screens, and breaks. KidsHealth, a US children's health site, says to let children do their own work and make their own mistakes.

  1. Home, snack, play. 30 to 60 minutes of free time first.
  2. Same time, same place. A kitchen table at 16:30 beats "whenever".
  3. Set a timer. Use the rule of thumb for their age. When it rings, stop.
  4. Be nearby, not on top. Ask "What do you need to do first?" rather than doing it.
  5. Write a note if it goes wrong. "He worked for 20 minutes and got stuck on question 3" tells the teacher more than a perfect sheet you finished.

Ask the teacher for a short chat if homework often ends in tears, your child is still very tired or upset after half term, or reading seems much harder than for classmates. KidsHealth notes some children need an eye test or an assessment for a learning difficulty. A simple opener: "I wanted to share what we see at home, and hear what you see in class." The NHS suggests meeting the teacher or the special educational needs coordinator (SENCO) if worries affect school life. If you are running on empty yourself, see our tired parent guide.

The short version

  • Tiredness, tears and meltdowns after school are common in the first term. Parents often report things easing by half term or after Christmas.
  • After-school restraint collapse is not a diagnosis. Snack, quiet time and movement come before questions.
  • Children do not need to read or write before school. Toileting, dressing and asking for help matter more.
  • In primary school, the evidence that homework helps is weak and mixed. About 10 minutes a night per US school grade (none in kindergarten) is a common rule of thumb, and quality beats quantity.
  • Reading together, briefly and warmly, is the most useful home learning. Support independence rather than doing the work.

When to ask your doctor or health visitor for help

  • Headaches or stomach aches come mostly on school days. Start with a check-up to rule out illness (AAP).
  • Your child refuses or avoids school for more than 1 week. HealthyChildren.org says you may need professional help at this point.
  • You notice changes in mood, sleep or appetite that last, or meltdowns keep disrupting family life (Henry Ford Health).
  • Anxiety is severe, does not ease and stops your child doing everyday things. The NHS says your GP (family doctor) is a good first step. In the UK, the YoungMinds parents helpline on 0808 802 5544 is free, though not a crisis service. Check its opening hours on the website.
  • If your child talks about wanting to hurt themselves, contact your GP the same day, or in the UK call NHS 111 and choose the mental health option. If they have hurt themselves, a life is at risk or you cannot keep them safe, call 999 in the UK or 112 anywhere in the European Union (EU) now. Childline (0800 1111) is free for under-19s in the UK.

Screens, games and the first phone from 5 to 10: why health bodies dropped the magic number

You have heard "1 hour a day", "2 hours a day" and "no smartphone before 14". Your child says everyone else already has one. Here is what health bodies and research actually say, how game ratings work, and how to decide on a first phone.

Most of her class got one in Year 5: what parents are asking now

In a recent Mumsnet thread, a parent asked when to give her Year 6 daughter a phone. Most friends got one at the start of Year 5, when they began walking home alone. Replies split. Some suggested a basic phone for calls and texts. Others set firm rules, such as no large group chats and no phone after 7pm.

The UK regulator Ofcom, in its report of 7 May 2025, says that by age 11, 91% of children own their own mobile phone.

  • Gaming: 41% of 6 to 7 year olds and 63% of 8 to 9 year olds play games online.
  • Social media: 40% of under-13s have a profile on a social media app or site.
  • Worrying content: 33% of 8 to 17 year olds saw something worrying or nasty online in the past 12 months.

Treat these as a signal, not proof of harm.

What the official bodies say, side by side

Clear limits exist for under-5s. For school-age children, most health bodies have dropped a single number. For the early years, see our screens guide for under-5s.

BodyAgeWhat it says
World Health Organization (WHO), April 2019Under 5No sedentary screen time under 2. At 2 to 4, no more than 1 hour a day, and less is better.
WHO, November 20205 to 17At least 60 minutes a day of moderate to vigorous activity on average. Limit recreational screen time, with no number set. Evidence rated low certainty.
American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), January and June 2026School ageNo daily hour limit. Focus on quality, context and conversation. Keep screens out of bedrooms and mealtimes, and out of the hour before bed. Delay personal tablets.
Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health (RCPCH), January 2019All childrenEvidence is too weak to set a cut-off. Families should agree limits based on the child. Avoid screens for 1 hour before planned bedtime.
Slovenian paediatricians, May 2021, on the site of the National Institute of Public Health (NIJZ)6 to 12On average at most 1 hour of leisure screens a day at 6 to 9, and 1.5 hours at 10 to 12. No own smartphone or tablet at 6 to 9, but a basic phone is fine.

The AAP policy statement appeared in the journal Pediatrics on 20 January 2026. It says screen use cannot be judged by time limits alone. It asks what screens push out, such as sleep and play.

Instead of a limit, the RCPCH suggests 4 checks: whether screen time is under control, whether it gets in the way of family life, whether it affects sleep, and whether snacking is under control. If you are happy with all 4, it says you are likely doing as well as you can.

So if a friend quotes an official 2-hour rule for 8 year olds, no current UK or US health body sets one. Slovenian paediatricians are the exception, and their limit is lower, at 1 hour.

What the research shows, with the honest small print

The largest overview is a 2024 review led by Taren Sanders in Nature Human Behaviour. It combined 102 meta-analyses, covering 2,451 studies and 1,937,501 children and young people. Most links between screens and outcomes were small to moderate.

The type of use mattered more than the total. General screen use was linked with weaker literacy. When parents watched with their children, the link turned positive. The authors warn that 95 of the 102 meta-analyses had a medium to high risk of bias.

Sleep is where the evidence is most consistent. A 2016 meta-analysis by Ben Carter and colleagues in JAMA Pediatrics pooled data on 125,198 children aged 6 to 19. Bedtime device use was linked with about double the odds of too little sleep. Even an unused device in the bedroom was linked with poorer sleep. The authors rated the evidence as low quality.

Most of this research cannot prove cause. Children who sleep badly or feel low may use screens more. For more on sleep at this age, see our school-age sleep guide.

Gaming and PEGI age ratings: what the labels mean

Games in Europe carry a Pan European Game Information (PEGI) label, used in more than 30 countries. The UK Games Rating Authority explains them.

LabelWhat it may contain
PEGI 3Suitable for all ages. Very mild, unrealistic violence in a child-like setting.
PEGI 7Unrealistic violence against fantasy characters. Scenes that may scare young children.
PEGI 12Moderate violence against humans without blood, milder swearing, moderate horror.
PEGI 16Realistic violence with blood, strong language, drugs and alcohol.
PEGI 18Adults only. Can include torture, sexual violence and glamorised gambling.

Nintendo's UK parent guide lists content descriptors on the box, including fear, gambling and in-game purchases. It says that in the UK, supplying a game rated PEGI 12, 16 or 18 to someone under that age is a criminal offence.

A rating does not tell you whether strangers can chat with your child, so check the chat settings yourself.

A 2022 study by Bader Chaarani in JAMA Network Open looked at 2,217 children aged 9 to 10. Those who played at least 21 hours a week did slightly better on impulse control and working memory tests. It was retracted and replaced on 10 April 2023 because of errors. The corrected version also found higher scores for attention problems, depression and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) among gamers. It is a snapshot study, so it cannot show cause.

The first phone: what the evidence can and cannot tell you

The AAP says studies do not point to a specific safe age. It suggests checking whether your child is mostly truthful and handles conflicts with friends safely.

  • A US cohort of 10,588 children. A study led by Ran Barzilay appeared in Pediatrics on 1 December 2025. The median age of the first smartphone was 11. Owning a smartphone at 12 was linked with higher odds of depression (1.31 times), obesity (1.40 times) and too little sleep (1.62 times). These are links only, adjusted for socioeconomic factors, puberty and parental monitoring.
  • A smaller study that found no link. A Stanford team followed more than 250 low-income Latino children in California, aged 7 to 11 at the start, with yearly checks until they were 11 to 15, reported in Child Development in 2022. The age of the first phone was not meaningfully linked with grades, sleep or depression symptoms. The sample was narrow.
  • A new UK signal on delaying together. The Smartphone Free Childhood campaign asks families to sign a Parent Pact: no smartphone before 14 and no social media before 16. It reports more than 100,000 pacts. A July 2026 working paper for the Institute for Fiscal Studies (IFS) compared English primary schools where at least 20% of families signed with schools under 5%. School-recorded mental health needs fell by about 8.2%. The fall in child psychiatric disability claims was largest at ages 5 to 10, about 8.7%. It is not peer-reviewed, it counts sign-ups rather than phones, and the schools were more affluent than average.

In our reading, the evidence leans towards caution, but it is not settled. We think the useful question is what your child needs the phone for. Match the device to the need.

School phone bans: what changed in 2025 and 2026

In England, Department for Education (DfE) guidance on phones in schools became statutory on 29 June 2026, for use from 1 September 2026. It says all schools should be mobile phone-free by default. That covers lessons, breaktimes and lunchtime.

In Slovenia, an amendment to the Primary School Act approved in June 2025 bans pupils' devices in primary school lessons and breaks, from the school year starting 1 September 2025. Teachers can allow use for school work, and health needs are exempt.

So a young child's phone is mostly for the journey home. See our screens and school guide.

Parental controls and calm rules that work

Built-in tools are free. Apple's Screen Time offers schedules, daily time allowances, approval before downloads and limits on who can message or call. Google Family Link, for children under 13 on Android, offers daily limits, a device bedtime, app approvals and location.

Controls are a backstop. The AAP pairs them with its free Family Media Plan, for agreeing rules as a family.

  1. Agree the rules before the device arrives. In our view, starting strict and relaxing later is easier than the reverse.
  2. Keep bedrooms and bedtime screen-free. Charge every device, including yours, outside the bedrooms overnight. The AAP and the Slovenian guidelines both suggest screen-free bedrooms, and the RCPCH advises no screens in the hour before bed.
  3. Set screen-free times. The AAP suggests starting with meals and homework. Try: "Phones go in the basket at dinner. Mine too."
  4. Turn off autoplay and notifications. The AAP says these features are designed to keep children engaged longer.
  5. Choose the content, and sometimes watch or play with them. In the Sanders review, co-viewing was linked with better literacy. Ask: "Show me the best bit of your game."
  6. Swap time limits for a daily rhythm. Screens after homework and outdoor play, then off. WHO's 60 active minutes a day is a useful anchor. Our active play guide has ideas.
  7. Make the phone a loan. A Mumsnet parent idea: "This phone is ours. You can use it while the rules work."
  8. Keep talking about what they see. "If anything online makes you feel funny, scared or confused, tell me. You will not be in trouble." See our online safety guide.

When your child says everyone else has one, try: "I know it feels unfair. We will look again in Year 6. Until then, you can call Grandma from my phone."

The short version

  • WHO, the AAP and the RCPCH set no daily hour limit after age 5. Slovenian paediatricians suggest 1 hour at 6 to 9 and 1.5 hours at 10 to 12.
  • What screens push out matters most: sleep, activity, homework and time together.
  • Screens out of the bedroom at night, and off 1 hour before bed, is the rule most bodies agree on.
  • No study proves a safe age for a first smartphone. A basic phone covers most early needs.
  • Controls help. The AAP pairs them with a written family plan and regular talk.

When to ask your doctor or health visitor for help

  • Screens or gaming often cut your child's sleep, or they are tired at school most days.
  • For several weeks, your child drops friends or hobbies, or is very upset whenever a device is taken away.
  • Your child seems low, anxious or withdrawn, or the school raises concerns about mood or attention.
  • Your child is being bullied online, or seems scared after using a device. Talk to the school the same week. UK children can call Childline free on 0800 1111, day or night. Parents can call the National Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children (NSPCC) helpline on 0808 800 5000. In Slovenia, children can call TOM, the free children's helpline, on 116 111, every day from 12 noon to 8pm. See our friends and bullying guide.
  • An adult asks your child for photos, secrets or a meeting. Keep the messages and report it the same day, through the Child Exploitation and Online Protection (CEOP) Safety Centre in the UK or to the police on 113 in Slovenia. If your child is in immediate danger, call 999 in the UK or 112 across Europe.

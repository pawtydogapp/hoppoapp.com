My baby cries a lot

All babies cry, and some cry much more than others. Long crying spells in a healthy baby are often called colic.

What is common

  • Colic means a healthy baby cries for more than 3 hours a day, on 3 or more days a week, for at least 1 week.
  • It usually starts when a baby is a few weeks old and stops by 3 to 4 months.
  • Crying is often worse in the late afternoon and evening.
  • During a spell, babies may go red, clench their fists, pull up their knees or arch their back.
  • Hunger, a dirty nappy, trapped wind and tiredness are common reasons for crying too.

What can help

  • Hold and cuddle your baby, and rock them gently over your shoulder or in a pram.
  • Keep your baby upright during feeds, and burp them after feeds.
  • A warm bath or calm background noise can help some babies settle.
  • Keep feeding as usual. Breastfeeding mothers do not need to change their diet for colic.
  • If you feel overwhelmed, put your baby down safely in the cot, leave the room for a few minutes and breathe. Ask someone to take over if you can.
  • Never shake a baby. It can cause serious brain injury.
  • Ask your doctor before trying colic drops, or herbal or homeopathic remedies. Most have no proven benefit.

Talk to your doctor, midwife or health visitor if

  • You are worried about the crying, or nothing seems to help.
  • You find it hard to cope.
  • Your baby is not gaining weight as expected.
  • Long crying spells continue after 4 months.
  • Your baby seems pale, or keeps vomiting.

Get urgent help now if

  • You feel you might hurt your baby. Put your baby down safely in their cot and get help now.
  • The cry is weak, high-pitched or does not sound like your baby's normal cry.
  • Your baby has other worrying signs, such as a high temperature, floppiness or trouble breathing.

Call your local emergency number or go to the nearest emergency department.

This guide is general information, not medical advice. Trust your instinct. If you are worried, contact your doctor, midwife or health visitor. This guide is checked against the NHS, the World Health Organization (WHO), the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) and other public health sources.

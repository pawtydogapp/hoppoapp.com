How should my baby sleep safely?

A few simple habits lower the risk of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS). They matter most in the first 6 months and apply for the whole first year.

What is common

  • Babies sleep safest on their back, in their own cot or Moses basket, in your room.
  • Once babies can roll both ways on their own, you do not need to turn them back.
  • A room temperature of about 16 to 20°C is comfortable for most babies.

What can help

  • Put your baby on their back for every sleep, with their feet at the foot of the cot.
  • Keep the cot in your room for at least the first 6 months, day and night.
  • Use a firm, flat mattress. Keep the cot clear: no pillows, duvets, bumpers, soft toys, pods or nests.
  • Use light bedding or a baby sleeping bag. Feel their chest or the back of their neck to check they are not too hot.
  • Take off hats and extra layers when you come indoors or get into the car.
  • Keep the home and car smoke-free.
  • A dummy at sleep times can lower the risk. If you breastfeed, wait until feeding is going well before you offer one.
  • If you swaddle, always lay your baby on their back, keep the wrap loose at the hips, and stop when your baby starts trying to roll.
  • Do not use sleep positioners, inclined sleepers, weighted blankets or weighted sleeping bags.
  • Never fall asleep with your baby on a sofa or armchair.
  • Do not share a bed if your baby was born early or small, or if anyone in the bed smokes, has drunk alcohol, used drugs or taken medicine that makes them drowsy.
  • Do not leave your baby sleeping in a car seat for long after a journey.

Talk to your doctor, midwife or health visitor if

  • You have questions about slings, swaddling or bed sharing.
  • You are worried about how your baby sleeps or breathes.

Get urgent help now if

  • Your baby is hard to wake, floppy, very pale or blue, or has trouble breathing. Call your emergency number.

Call your local emergency number or go to the nearest emergency department.

This guide is general information, not medical advice. Trust your instinct. If you are worried, contact your doctor, midwife or health visitor. This guide is checked against the NHS, the World Health Organization (WHO), the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) and other public health sources.

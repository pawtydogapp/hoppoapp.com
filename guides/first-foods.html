When does my baby start solid food?

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends starting solid food at around 6 months. Some European health services, including the one in Slovenia, say between 4 and 6 months, but never before 17 weeks. Ask your paediatrician or health visitor what is right for your baby.

What is common

  • A baby is ready when 3 signs appear together: they can sit with their head steady, pick up food and put it in their mouth, and swallow food instead of pushing it back out.
  • Chewing fists, waking at night or wanting more milk are normal and do not mean your baby is ready.
  • Gagging is common while babies learn to eat. Watery eyes and pushing food out are part of learning.
  • A new food can take 10 tries or more before your baby accepts it.

What can help

  • Start with a few spoonfuls or soft pieces of single vegetables or fruit, such as broccoli, carrot, apple or pear.
  • Offer finger food about the size of your finger, soft enough to squash.
  • Give sips of water from an open cup with meals.
  • Introduce common allergens, such as egg, peanut, gluten and fish, one at a time in small amounts.
  • Always stay with your baby while they eat.
  • Do not add salt or sugar. No honey before 1 year, and no cow's milk as the main drink before 1 year.
  • Avoid whole nuts, hard raw foods and whole grapes or cherry tomatoes. Cut small round foods lengthways into quarters.

Talk to your doctor, midwife or health visitor if

  • Your baby was born early. Ask when to start.
  • Your baby has a reaction to a food, such as a rash.

Get urgent help now if

  • Your baby is choking and cannot cough, cry or breathe. Call your emergency number and start first aid.
  • Swelling of the lips or face, or trouble breathing, after a new food. Call your emergency number.

Call your local emergency number or go to the nearest emergency department.

This guide is general information, not medical advice. Trust your instinct. If you are worried, contact your doctor, midwife or health visitor. This guide is checked against the NHS, the World Health Organization (WHO), the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) and other public health sources.

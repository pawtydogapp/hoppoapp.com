Up to 8 sibling fights an hour: how to referee fairly, set house rules and handle pocket money and lies

Your children love each other. They also fight over the front seat and the last biscuit. Here is what research says about how common it is, when it becomes bullying, how to step in fairly, and how to handle chores, pocket money and lies.

Up to 8 fights an hour: you are not doing it wrong

In December 2024, the US family researcher Laurie Kramer said that siblings aged 4 to 8 can have up to 8 fights an hour. The article does not say how this was measured, so treat it as an expert estimate.

A US study by Corinna Jenkins Tucker and others, in Pediatrics in June 2013, covered nearly 3,600 children aged 1 month to 17 years with at least 1 sibling. In the past year, 32% had faced at least 1 type of sibling aggression, such as being hit, having things broken or being called names.

In August 2026, a parent on Mumsnet described brothers aged 8 and 10 whose teasing had turned into physical fights. In July 2026, another asked how much pocket money a 7 year old should get. We read both threads in November 2026.

Normal rivalry or sibling bullying: how to tell

Most sibling fights flare up and end. Childline Ireland describes bullying as intentional, repeated aggression towards someone who finds it hard to defend themselves.

Usually normal rivalryPossible warning sign of bullying
Both children start fights, and both win some.The same child is always the target.
Rows are about something, such as a toy, a turn or a seat.The aim seems to be to hurt, scare or humiliate.
Children make up quickly and still play together.One child seems afraid, avoids the other or does not want to be left alone with them.
Fights are loud but rarely physical.Hitting, kicking or pushing is regular, or causes injuries.

The table is Hoppo's summary of Childline Ireland's signs and a 2015 review in The Lancet Psychiatry by Dieter Wolke and colleagues. That review estimated that up to 40% of children are exposed to sibling bullying every week. Parenting quality and behaviour were the family factors most strongly linked with it.

The long-term studies show links, not proof. A UK study by Lucy Bowes and others, in Pediatrics in 2014, followed more than 6,900 children. Children often bullied by a sibling at age 12 had about twice the odds of depression and self-harm at age 18. A 2021 UK study of more than 17,000 young people linked more frequent sibling bullying at 11 and 14 with poorer mental health at 17, for those who bullied as well as those bullied.

Young age does not make it harmless. A 2006 US survey of 2,030 children aged 2 to 17, by David Finkelhor and colleagues, found that younger children's sibling victimisation was no less serious than older children's. Children bullied at home and at school had markedly more emotional problems, Wolke's review found. For school bullying, see our friends and bullying guide.

How to referee without taking sides

For small squabbles, stepping back is often fine. For bigger rows, helping children solve the problem seems to work better than ruling on it.

In 2007, Jennifer Smith and Hildy Ross at the University of Waterloo in Canada trained parents in 48 families with children aged 5 to 10 to mediate sibling disputes. Compared with usual parenting, conflicts ended more fairly and children got better at seeing their sibling's view. It was a small study.

A March 2025 leaflet from Aneurin Bevan University Health Board, part of NHS Wales, gives a clear sequence. Here it is as 5 steps.

  1. Check safety first. If anyone is hitting or about to, separate the children calmly: "I will not let you hurt each other. You go to the sofa, you go to the kitchen table."
  2. Name the feelings. The NHS Wales leaflet suggests phrases like "I bet that gets you really annoyed." Kramer suggests that "I hate you" often means "I am frustrated".
  3. Let each child speak, then sum up both sides. "So Maja, you were building the tower, and Luka, you wanted a turn with the blocks." Kramer's phrase "see it your way, see it my way" helps children slow down.
  4. Describe the problem neutrally. "There is 1 box of blocks and 2 builders." No verdict on who started it.
  5. Ask them for a solution, then help agree a repair. "What could work for both of you?" If they cannot agree, offer 2 choices, such as a 10 minute timer or playing apart for a while.

The leaflet also says not to label any child "the bully". Criticise the behaviour, not the child, and praise the times they sort things out alone. In a 2008 randomised study, 95 families with children aged 4 to 8 tried Kramer's "More Fun with Sisters and Brothers" programme. Parents then reported more warmth and less rivalry between the children.

Fair is not equal: what children actually accept

In a 2002 study, Amanda Kowal, Laurie Kramer and colleagues interviewed children from 135 US families. The younger siblings were about 11 to 13, older than this article's focus. Children rated unequal treatment as fair in about 74% of cases. Those who saw the difference as fair tended to have higher self-worth and fewer worried or low feelings.

A 2025 meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin by Alexander Jensen and McKell Jorgensen-Wells combined data on 19,469 people. It found that both mothers and fathers tended to favour daughters, as well as children who were more responsible and organised. Less-favoured siblings tend to have poorer mental health, the authors say. The work is correlational.

What helps, according to these sources:

  • Give each child time based on need, not by the minute. The NHS Wales leaflet says this directly.
  • Explain the reason out loud. "Your sister goes to bed later because she is 9. You will too, when you are 9."
  • Avoid comparisons. The leaflet says comparing children can make them feel they compete for your approval.

Family meetings and a few clear house rules

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), on its HealthyChildren.org site, suggests a weekly family meeting of about 20 to 30 minutes, for example after dinner. The advice dates from February 2022.

The AAP's ground rules are simple. Each person speaks without being criticised or interrupted. The meeting is not a place to scold, punish or bring up old mistakes. Parents have the final word on hard decisions. Set goals for the week, note good efforts and write down what was agreed.

In our opinion, 4 or 5 rules, written together at a meeting and phrased as what to do, are easier to keep than a long list of bans. For example:

  • We keep our hands and feet to ourselves.
  • We ask before we borrow, and we give it back.
  • We use words, not names, when we are cross.
  • We each help at home.

Chores and pocket money: what the numbers say

The UK money guidance website MoneyHelper says children's money habits are largely set by age 7. Its age guide suggests that at 5 to 6, children can learn wants versus needs and why people save. At 7 to 8, they can set a savings goal. At 9 to 11, they can start to budget their own pocket money. For 5 to 6 year olds, it suggests small amounts of pocket money.

The NatWest Rooster Money Pocket Money Index 2026 gives UK averages from more than half a million card users, March 2025 to February 2026. Treat it as a signal, not a study. App users may not be typical.

Making the bed was the most common chore, at an average of 0.28 GBP a time. Among families with a regular allowance, 34.8% make it depend on chores.

In Slovenia, an April 2025 article on Svet24 said experts suggest starting around age 10, and saving 10 to 20%. There is no single right age or amount.

On paying for chores, the AAP says it depends on your aim. If you pay for jobs, say exactly what earns what, and stick to it. The AAP also says school-age children are not ready to pay for necessities such as clothes.

We suggest keeping everyday family jobs unpaid and paying a little for bigger jobs, as several parents in the Mumsnet thread do. A 2022 study led by Deanna Tepper at La Trobe University in Australia, of just over 200 parents of children aged 5 to 13, linked more household chores with better working memory and self-control. It only shows a link. Organised children may simply get more jobs. For saving jars and first accounts, see our pocket money guide.

Lying: why calm beats harsh

Almost every child lies sometimes. The AAP says that before about age 6, children often cannot clearly separate fact from fantasy. After about 6, a lie is usually deliberate, most often to avoid trouble or to avoid disappointing a parent.

Harsh punishment does not seem to make children more honest. In a 2015 study by Victoria Talwar and colleagues at McGill University in Canada, 372 children aged 4 to 8 were asked not to peek at a toy. 67.5% peeked, and 66.5% of those then lied about it. The threat of punishment did not stop the lying. Children told the truth more often when they were told it would please the adult.

A 2011 study by Talwar and Kang Lee compared 2 West African schools, with children aged 3 and 4. In the school that beat children, nearly all who peeked then lied. In the school that used time-outs, just over half did.

What the AAP and these studies suggest:

  • Say calmly that you know. "I think the vase broke when you were playing ball. I am not cross that it broke. I want to hear what happened."
  • Make the truth the easier path. "If you tell me the truth, we will sort it out together." Keep the consequence small, such as helping to clear up.
  • Praise honesty itself. "Thank you for telling me. That was hard, and you did it."
  • Watch your own white lies. The AAP notes that children notice them.

Our lying guide has more ideas. If you are running on empty, see the tired parent guide.

The short version

  • Sibling conflict is very common. In 1 large US survey, 32% of children faced sibling aggression in a year.
  • Rivalry goes both ways and ends quickly. Bullying is repeated, aimed at the same child and meant to hurt, and it is linked with later mental health problems.
  • For big rows, help children solve the problem instead of judging who started it. Name feelings, sum up both sides and ask for a solution.
  • Fair is not equal. Children accept differences when you explain the reason.
  • Keep pocket money small and clear, and meet lies with calm, not fear.

When to ask your doctor or health visitor for help

  • One child is always the target, seems afraid of a sibling, or does not want to be left alone with them.
  • Physical fights happen most days, cause injuries, or involve objects that could hurt.
  • A child seems low, anxious or withdrawn, sleeps badly, or talks about hurting themselves. If a child talks about wanting to die, contact your doctor the same day, or call your local emergency number if they are in immediate danger.
  • Sexual touching or play between siblings that worries you. Speak to your doctor or a child protection service promptly.
  • Lying is constant and comes with other worries at home or school. The AAP advises an assessment by a counsellor or mental health professional.
  • You are so angry or exhausted that you worry how you will react. Make sure the children are safe, step away and ask for support.
  • Children can get help themselves. In the UK, Childline is free on 0800 1111. In Ireland, Childline is on 1800 66 66 66. In Slovenia, TOM telefon is on 116 111, free, from 12:00 to 20:00. If a child is injured or in danger, call your local emergency number.

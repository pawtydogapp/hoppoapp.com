Puberty can start at 8: how to talk about bodies, periods and privacy before it begins

Many parents plan "the talk" for age 11 or 12. For some children that is 3 or 4 years too late. Here are the normal age ranges, the signs that mean a call to the doctor, the 5 PANTS body safety rules, period and hygiene basics, and calm answers to awkward questions.

Body odour at 7, breast buds at 8: what parents are asking

In June 2025, a Mumsnet thread titled "8 year old starting puberty?" drew 53 replies. The parent described underarm hair, spots, greasy hair and body odour in a slim, sporty 8 year old. A puberty book had told her that first signs come at about 10. She wondered whether to see the general practitioner (GP), and worried about embarrassing her daughter.

Mumsnet's preteens board, read on 1 November 2026, shows the same worries. 2026 threads ask about a 7 year old with body odour and tummy aches at 9 as a first sign.

Children worry too. On Childline's website, a 12 year old in puberty since about 8 writes that she feels different and gets unwanted attention.

A 2020 UK survey linked to a WaterAid campaign asked more than 1,000 people aged 14 to 21. 64% of young women knew very little or nothing about periods before their first one. 23% felt scared when it started. Treat this as a signal, not a study.

This article covers the talking. For the body changes step by step, see our puberty guide.

When puberty usually starts, and in what order

The National Health Service (NHS) says puberty can start any time between 8 and 14. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) and Nemours KidsHealth, a US children's health site, give ranges by sex.

By the AAP figures, 2 girls in 1 class can start puberty up to 5 years apart, and both can be normal.

Is puberty starting earlier? What the data shows

For girls, the answer appears to be yes. In 2020 the journal JAMA Pediatrics published a review by Camilla Eckert-Lind and colleagues. It pooled 30 studies from around the world, with examinations from 1977 to 2013. The age at which breasts start to develop fell by about 3 months per decade.

The small print: 79% of the 38 studies in the review were snapshots in time, and many did not record body mass index (BMI), which strongly affects when puberty starts.

The Copenhagen Puberty Study, published in Pediatrics in 2009, compared 1,100 girls examined in 1991 to 1993 with 995 girls examined in 2006 to 2008. The average age at first breast development fell from 10.88 to 9.86 years. The average age at first period fell much less, from 13.42 to 13.13.

A 2025 review of 26 studies from 11 countries found that 25 reported more early puberty in girls during the COVID-19 pandemic, 1.3 to 5 times more. Results in boys were mixed. The authors say part of the rise may reflect parents noticing more and seeking care more.

Older leaflets can give later ages. A 2007 youth booklet from the body now called Slovenia's National Institute of Public Health (NIJZ) gives 10 to 14 for girls.

A 2017 review of 101 studies in Psychological Bulletin linked early puberty with slightly more emotional and behaviour problems in adolescence, in girls and boys. The effects were small, and the studies show a link, not a cause. Preparing early starters is 1 thing parents can do.

Normal, or a reason to see the doctor

The NHS says signs of puberty before age 8 could be early puberty, and that no signs by 15 could be delayed puberty. NHS Scotland's referral guidance for doctors, reviewed in January 2024, says to suspect early puberty in a girl under 8 or a boy under 9. The AAP uses the same ages. KidsHealth uses before 7 for girls. If unsure, ask your GP.

  • Often a normal variant: body odour, underarm hair or a little pubic hair, with no breast or genital growth. Before 8 in girls or 9 in boys, the AAP calls this premature adrenarche. It says it generally causes no health problems, and that deodorants are safe. A doctor can confirm which it is.
  • More likely true early puberty: breast growth that keeps going in a girl under 8, or growth of the testicles or penis in a boy under 9, especially with a fast growth spurt.
  • Boys get checked more fully. NHS Scotland notes that early puberty with no clear cause is common in girls but rare in boys.

The NHS says treatment is usually only needed if early puberty causes physical or emotional problems. The AAP describes medicines, given by injection or implant, that can pause puberty.

How to start the conversation: 7 tools that help

  1. Start before the body does. KidsHealth advises talking before the changes begin, and notes that some girls start at 8 and some boys by 9. In England, the Department for Education's 2025 guidance tells primary schools to teach about periods before they start, and says periods can start at 8.
  2. Talk little and often. The AAP advises talking early and often. In our view, 10 short chats beat 1 big talk.
  3. Ask before you answer. The AAP suggests finding out exactly what the question is first. Try "What have you heard about that?"
  4. Use the real names. The same English guidance lists penis, vulva, vagina, testicles, scrotum and nipples as words children should learn by the end of primary school. In our view, calm and correct words make it easier for a child to tell you about pain, an itch or an unwanted touch.
  5. Use everyday moments. The National Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children (NSPCC) suggests bath time, getting dressed, car journeys and a storyline on television.
  6. Tell boys about periods and girls about wet dreams. KidsHealth advises covering both. In the 2020 survey, 34% of boys and young men were still uninformed about periods.
  7. Check what school teaches. In England, parents cannot withdraw children from relationships education, health education or puberty in science. They can ask to withdraw them only from sex education, and primary schools must agree. Ask when lessons happen, so you can talk first.

Privacy and body safety: the PANTS rule

The NSPCC teaches 5 simple rules under the name PANTS, with a cartoon dinosaur called Pantosaurus. In short:

  • P, privates are private. Underwear covers your private parts, and nobody should ask to see or touch them.
  • A, always remember your body belongs to you.
  • N, no means no. Your child has the right to say no, even to a family member or someone they love.
  • T, talk about secrets that upset you. There are good secrets and bad secrets.
  • S, speak up, someone can help. Talk about anything that makes you worried or upset.

The NSPCC parent guide adds 1 exception children need to hear. Doctors or nurses may sometimes need to touch private parts, and they should always ask first and explain why.

A 2015 Cochrane review by Kerryann Walsh and colleagues pooled 24 trials with 5,802 primary school children. School programmes improved knowledge of body safety, for about 6 months in the 4 trials that checked. 3 trials found no rise in anxiety or fear. The effect on telling an adult about abuse was uncertain, and no study measured whether abuse fell.

Hoppo's view: when your child wants to wash and dress alone, let them. Knock before you enter their room, and ask before you share their photos. That shows their body and image belong to them. Our online safety guide covers privacy on screens.

Periods, sweat and the awkward questions

The NHS says a period is about 20 to 90 ml of blood, roughly 1 to 5 tablespoons. A first period often shows as a reddish-brown stain in underwear, not a sudden flow. The NHS suggests pads for a very first period, as tampons and cups can take some getting used to.

Hoppo's view: put 2 pads, clean pants and a wet wipe in a zipped pouch in the school bag. Tell your child which teacher to ask for help.

For sweat, an NHS school health service in Milton Keynes advises a daily shower, clean clothes and drying feet well. A roll-on or spray deodorant is fine. The AAP says deodorants are safe for children with early body odour.

A calm answer of 2 or 3 sentences is usually enough. These are examples, not scripts.

Your child asksYou could say
"What is a period?""About once a month, the lining of the womb comes out through the vagina as a little blood. It means the body is growing up healthily. A pad in your pants catches it."
"Why has my friend got boobs and I have not?""Every body has its own timetable. Some start at 8 and some at 13. Both are normal."

The short version

  • Puberty usually starts between 8 and 13 in girls and 9 and 14 in boys. First periods come on average at about 12, but can come at 8.
  • Signs before 8 in a girl or 9 in a boy are worth a GP visit. Body odour and a little hair alone are often a normal variant.
  • Girls' breast development has started earlier over recent decades, by about 3 months per decade in a review of 30 studies.
  • Talk little and often, use the real names of body parts, and tell every child about both sexes.
  • Teach the 5 PANTS rules. Your child's body belongs to them, and worrying secrets are always safe to tell.

When to ask your doctor or health visitor for help

  • Breast growth in a girl under 8, or growth of the testicles or penis in a boy under 9 (NHS Scotland, AAP).
  • Pubic hair, underarm hair or adult body odour before these ages, even alone. It is often harmless, but ask your GP to check.
  • Very fast growth, or outgrowing clothes much faster than usual, with signs of puberty.
  • Early signs of puberty together with headaches, being sick, changes in vision or great thirst. NHS Scotland lists these as warning signs. Ask for a same-day GP appointment.
  • No signs of puberty by 15, or no periods by 15, or by 13 if there are no other signs of puberty (NHS).
  • Your child seems very anxious, low or withdrawn about their body, or is teased about it.
  • If your child tells you about an unwanted touch, stay calm. The NSPCC advises telling them it is not their fault and they will not get into trouble. If they are in danger, call 999 in the UK or 112 across Europe. In the UK, the NSPCC Helpline is 0808 800 5000, and children can call Childline free on 0800 1111.

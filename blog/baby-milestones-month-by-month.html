Baby milestones month by month: the real ranges from first smile to first hop, and when to ask

The baby at your group is crawling at 7 months, your cousin's son walked at 10, and yours is happily sitting and watching at 11 months. If you have lain awake doing the maths, you are in good company. Here are the real age ranges from the World Health Organization (WHO), what 3 in 4 children do at each age from birth to 5, and the signs that are always worth a call.

58% of worried parents compare their child with a friend's

Milestone worry rarely starts with a chart. It usually starts with 1 other child.

The C.S. Mott Children's Hospital National Poll on Children's Health asked US parents about this in January 2021. Of 779 parents with a child aged 0 to 5, 38% had compared their child with a sibling and 34% with a friend's children. Among parents who had worried about a delay, 58% had compared with friends' children. Among parents who had not worried, it was 27%.

Many parents on Mumsnet describe a baby at group who is already crawling and pulling to stand, and then ask what they are doing wrong. Others describe the same race starting again with their second baby, often after comments from relatives.

A study from Zurich, Switzerland, followed 222 healthy children. They started walking anywhere between 8.5 and 20 months, at 12 months on average. Children who walked early were neither more intelligent nor better coordinated later on (Jenni and colleagues, Acta Paediatrica, 2013).

Slovenia's National Institute of Public Health (NIJZ) puts it simply in its 2026 guide for new parents. Professionals never judge a delay on movement or milestones alone. They look at the whole child. Its advice to parents is not to compare their child with others, and to talk to the child's paediatrician if they are worried.

How to read a milestone: a window, and a 3-in-4 age

The big sources give 2 kinds of number. Both reassure once you know what they mean.

WHO windows. The WHO Motor Development Study followed 816 healthy children in Ghana, India, Norway, Oman and the USA. For 6 movement milestones it published a "window of achievement". Each window runs from the 1st to the 99th percentile. So 98 in 100 healthy children reached the skill inside it.

MilestoneWHO window of achievement
Sitting without support3.8 to 9.2 months
Standing with help4.8 to 11.4 months
Crawling on hands and knees5.2 to 13.5 months
Walking with help5.9 to 13.7 months
Standing alone6.9 to 16.9 months
Walking alone8.2 to 17.6 months

CDC checklists. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) revised their milestone checklists in 2022, the first update since 2004. The old lists showed what about half of children could do at an age. The new ones show what 75% or more can do. A missed item is a reason to ask your doctor.

UK reviews. In England, the National Health Service (NHS) offers development reviews at 9 to 12 months and at 2 to 2.5 years, with a questionnaire whose activities you can try at home.

0 to 3 months: the first smile is a milestone

By 2 months, the CDC lists that most babies:

  • look at your face, and calm down when spoken to or picked up
  • smile when you talk to them or smile at them
  • make sounds other than crying
  • hold their head up when lying on their tummy

Try this week: hold your baby close and chat about what you are doing. Pause, and let them answer with a look or a sound.

4 to 6 months: rolling is on the 6-month list, not the 4-month one

By 4 months, most babies coo, turn their head towards your voice, push up onto their elbows on their tummy and hold their head steady when you hold them.

By 6 months, the CDC adds rolling from tummy to back, laughing, blowing raspberries and taking turns making sounds with you. Some babies already sit, since the WHO sitting window opens at 3.8 months.

Try this week: copy your baby's sounds back to them, then wait for a reply.

7 to 12 months: sitting, babbling and the crawling surprise

By 9 months, most babies sit without support and get into sitting by themselves. They babble "mamamama" or "babababa", lift their arms to be picked up, look when you call their name and may be shy with strangers.

By 12 months, the CDC lists pulling up to stand, walking while holding on to furniture, waving bye-bye, calling a parent "mama", "dada" or another special name, and understanding "no".

Crawling is not on the CDC checklists at any age. The CDC dropped it largely for lack of data, and 4.3% of babies in the WHO study never crawled on hands and knees. Some shuffle on their bottom or roll instead. NHS Healthier Together notes that bottom shufflers tend to walk later than crawlers. Our crawling guide covers the different styles.

Try this week: play peekaboo, and wave bye-bye every time someone leaves the room.

1 to 2 years: most first steps come between 8 and 18 months

Walking alone has the latest WHO window, from 8.2 to 17.6 months. The CDC lists taking a few steps alone at 15 months, and walking without holding on at 18 months.

By 15 months, most children try 1 or 2 words besides "mama" or "dada" and point to ask for something. By 18 months, they try 3 or more words, follow a simple instruction without a gesture and point to show you something interesting.

Walking is easy to see, so it gets compared most. Quieter skills, such as pointing, looking when you say their name and understanding simple words, are on the checklists too. They deserve the same attention.

By 2 years, most children run, kick a ball and put at least 2 words together, such as "more milk". The NHS says children start to put simple sentences together at around age 2.

Try this week: when your child points at something, say its name and give the word back.

2 to 3 years: about 50 words, then the first real chats

By 30 months, the CDC lists about 50 words, 2-word phrases with an action word such as "doggie run", words like "I", "me" and "we", and jumping with both feet.

By 3 years, most children hold a conversation with at least 2 back-and-forth exchanges, ask questions such as "what" or "where", say their first name and use a fork. Other people understand them most of the time.

On Mumsnet, 1 parent described a 2-year-old with plenty of single words who would not put them together. Later that same day, the child joined 2 words out of the blue. If you are unsure, ask anyway. Our talking guide has ideas to try at home.

Try this week: read a picture book and ask "what is that?" before you name each thing.

3 to 5 years: from stories to the first hop

By 4 years, the CDC lists sentences of 4 or more words, talking about at least 1 thing from their day, pretend play and catching a large ball most of the time.

By 5 years, most children hop on 1 foot, keep a conversation going with more than 3 back-and-forth exchanges, count to 10 and take turns in games with other children.

Try this week: at dinner, ask about 1 thing from their day. Then hop to the bath together.

3 myths that cause needless worry

"A baby walker will help them walk." The AAP says walkers can actually delay walking, and they send thousands of children to hospital every year. It has called for a ban on the manufacture and sale of baby walkers with wheels.

"Crawling is essential." Some therapists think crawling has benefits. But very few studies have looked at what skipping it means, and the CDC dropped it from its checklists.

"Bilingual children talk late, so drop a language." The NHS says lots of children grow up in a family with more than 1 language. It tells parents to talk in whichever language feels comfortable, even if each parent uses a different one. It adds that another language helps a child's English develop. The usual advice still applies: if you are worried about your child's speech in any language, ask.

The 30-second version

  • Milestones are windows. Walking alone ranges from 8.2 to 17.6 months for 98 in 100 children in WHO data.
  • CDC ages show what 3 in 4 children do. Missing 1 is a reason to ask, and no reason to panic.
  • Early walking did not predict intelligence or coordination in a Zurich study of 222 children.
  • Comparing with 1 other child tells you little. Watch your own child's progress over weeks.

When to talk to your doctor or health visitor

Reassurance and asking early go together. In the research behind the 2022 CDC checklists, parents of children with disabilities said they had been told to wait. These are worth asking about, named by source:

  • Lost skills, at any age. The CDC says to act early if your child has lost skills they once had. NHS Healthier Together adds walking that suddenly becomes very unsteady.
  • A missed milestone, or any worry at all. The CDC advises against waiting. Talk to your child's doctor and ask about developmental screening.
  • Movement (NHS Healthier Together). Poor head control at 4 months, not sitting without support by 9 months, not walking a few steps alone by 18 months, 1 side of the body moving differently or seeming weaker, walking only on tiptoes, walking that is still very unsteady 6 months after it starts, repetitive jerking, twitching or writhing movements, or seeming very stiff or very floppy. Repeated sudden jerks or spasms in a baby need a doctor the same day.
  • Hearing. If your baby does not startle at loud sounds or turn towards voices, ask for a hearing check. It is quick and easy to arrange.
  • Talking and social skills (NHS). If you are worried about your child's speech or language, talk to your GP or health visitor. In many areas you can also refer your child to a speech and language therapist yourself. The NHS lists signs such as not responding to their name, using eye contact less often and using fewer facial expressions and gestures as possible signs of autism to raise with a GP. Any concern about how your child communicates or connects with people is worth raising.
  • Between check-ups (NHS). You can contact a health visitor or GP, or go to your local baby clinic, whenever you have a concern.

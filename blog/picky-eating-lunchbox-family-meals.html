Picky eating at 5 to 10: fussiness halves by age 6, and 15 calm tries beat 1 mealtime battle

The lunchbox comes home full. Dinner is pasta again. You worry that your child is missing out, and that you are doing it wrong. Here is why fussiness peaks and fades, why repeat tasting beats pressure, what new school food rules say, and when to see a doctor.

12 safe foods and a lunch that comes back: what parents are asking

In September 2026, a parent on Mumsnet asked about a fussy eating specialist for a 7 year old. The boy ate a rotation of about 12 foods. After a holiday of pizza, crisps and burgers, his school clothes looked loose.

In July 2026, another parent wrote about a son of 4 and a half who was about to start school. He ate bread, nuggets, some chips, cucumber and certain fruit, and called most other food disgusting without trying it. Most of the 42 replies said the same thing: keep the pressure low, keep a safe food on the plate, and expect slow progress.

Most of these worries have a reassuring answer, and a few need a closer look. For mealtimes with younger children, see our picky eating guide.

Why fussiness peaks early, and eases for most children

Food neophobia is a reluctance to try new foods. Picky eating is rejecting many foods, including familiar ones. A 2008 review in the journal Appetite found that pressure to eat and feeding styles affect both.

Genes play a large part. In 2007, Lucy Cooke and colleagues at University College London studied 5,390 pairs of twins aged 8 to 11. They estimated that 78% of the differences in food neophobia were down to genes, and 22% to environment.

The timeline is the good news. A Dutch study of 4,018 children, published in 2015, asked mothers about picky eating at 3 ages.

AgeChildren described as picky eaters
1.5 years26.5%
3 years27.6%
6 years13.2%, about 1 in 8

55% of children were never picky, and 32% were picky early and then grew out of it. 4% became picky only at age 6, and 4% stayed picky at every age. Persistent picky eating was more common in boys, in children with a lower birth weight and in families with a low income.

The small print: the data are mothers' reports, and a link with income does not show cause. Still, for most children, fussiness is a phase that eases in the early school years.

10 to 15 tries: where the number comes from

The often quoted 10 to 15 tastes is usually traced to a 1990 US study by Sullivan and Birch. A 2006 summary by the French fruit and vegetable body Aprifel, citing that study, says older children and adults may need as many as 10 to 15 taste exposures. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) says on HealthyChildren.org that it can take children 15 to 20 tries to like a new food.

In a 2003 trial led by Jane Wardle, 49 children in 3 London schools were offered a small taste of red pepper every day, 8 times over 2 weeks. Compared with children who had no tastings, they liked the pepper more and ate more of it. A group who could pick a sticker for eating a piece did slightly better than no tastings, but not clearly so.

A 2018 review in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition by Katherine Appleton and colleagues pooled 43 articles on vegetables. Repeated exposure raised liking by about 0.6 points on a 9-point scale, and intake by about 10 g. The authors called the effects small, with limited evidence of long-term benefit.

So treat the number as a rough guide. The National Health Service (NHS) says it may take lots of attempts, and a child who hates a food 1 day may love it a month later. In our view, a try can be tiny: looking, touching, smelling or licking all count. Changing how a food is prepared helps too. The NHS example is raw grated carrot for a child who refuses cooked carrot.

Pressure, bribes and bans: what tends to backfire

In a 2006 US experiment by Amy Galloway and colleagues, 27 children aged 3 to 5 ate soup over 11 weeks. With 1 soup, an adult said "Finish your soup, please" 4 times in 5 minutes. With the other, nobody said anything. Intake rose more for the soup served without pressure. The children made 157 negative comments during pressure sessions, against 30 without.

Bans can backfire too. In a 1999 study, Jennifer Fisher and Leann Birch found that restricting a tasty snack made children more focused on it, and in 1 experiment they later chose and ate more of it.

The NHS advice on fussy eaters is written for toddlers. In our view, it fits older children too. Do not force food. Take rejected food away without comment and try again another time. Do not use sweets as a reward for eating. Use non-food rewards, such as a trip to the park.

  • Myth: "They will eat when they are hungry enough." Most children do. A child with a feeding disorder may not notice hunger well, which is why the warning signs below matter.
  • Myth: "A clean plate is a good habit." The Galloway study suggests pushing a child to finish can make a food less liked.
  • Myth: "Cook them their own meal." The AAP advises against it. Include 1 food your child likes instead.

The division of responsibility, in plain words

The US dietitian Ellyn Satter describes a model called the division of responsibility. From toddlers to teenagers, the adult decides what, when and where. The child decides how much, and whether to eat at all. It is 1 way to put responsive feeding into practice.

Adult's jobChild's job
Choose and prepare the foodEat the amount they need
Offer regular meals and snacksLearn to eat the food the family enjoys
Make mealtimes pleasantLearn to behave well at the table
Offer only water between meals and snacksDecide whether to eat what is served

Shared meals help. A 2018 meta-analysis by Mattea Dallacker and colleagues combined 57 studies with 203,706 participants. More frequent family meals were linked with better diet quality and a slightly lower body mass index (BMI). The links were small, and the studies cannot prove cause. The AAP also advises eating together without screens.

Sentences you can try at the table:

  1. "You do not have to eat it. It can just sit on your plate."
  2. "Your tummy decides how much. I decide what is for dinner."
  3. "Be a food scientist. Tell me if it is crunchy, soft, sweet or sour."
  4. "The bread is there if you are still hungry." A safe food takes the fear out of a new one.
  5. "Can you help me wash the peppers?" The AAP says children who help choose, grow and cook food are more likely to try it in the end.

The lunchbox and school food: what the rules say

A University of Leeds study in BMJ Open in 2020 checked packed lunches of children aged 8 to 9 in English primary schools. In 2006, 1.1% of 1,148 lunches met all 8 school meal standards. In 2016, 1.6% of 323 lunches did, a change that was not statistically significant. Lunches with a sweetened drink fell from 60% to 46%. The paper says Unilever partly funded the 2016 work. A 2017 Leeds summary added that only 17% held any vegetables or salad.

School meals in England are changing. The Department for Education published its consultation response in September 2026. Subject to Parliament, the new standards apply from September 2027.

  • Drinks: no fruit juice in school meals. Free drinking water at all times.
  • Puddings: primary schools are limited to 1 sweetened dessert or baked product per week. Secondary schools may serve 2.
  • Frying and sweeteners: no deep-fried food, and low- or no-calorie sweeteners restricted in primary schools.
  • Why: the government says children eat double the recommended sugar, and only 14% of primary-aged children meet fibre recommendations.

The standards cover food that schools serve. The response does not say they cover lunches packed at home, so check your school's food policy.

In Slovenia, the Guidelines for nutrition in educational institutions were adopted on 23 November 2023 and apply from the 2024/25 school year. Sweet drinks are on the list of items schools should not order. A 2026 report by the National Institute of Public Health (NIJZ) on 128 kindergartens, schools and other educational institutions found menus largely in line. Still, 20% served slightly sweetened tea.

Sugary drinks and snacks: the numbers that matter

The NHS sets daily limits for free sugars. These are sugars added to food and drink, plus the sugar in honey, syrups and unsweetened juices and smoothies.

  • Ages 4 to 6: no more than 19 g a day, about 5 sugar cubes.
  • Ages 7 to 10: no more than 24 g a day, about 6 sugar cubes.
  • Juice and smoothies: a maximum of 150 ml, a small glass, in total per day.

The UK charity The Food Foundation said in May 2026 that a 150 ml glass of fruit juice gives a primary-aged child about 75% of their daily maximum of free sugar.

The World Health Organization (WHO) fact sheet of 26 January 2026 says free sugars should be below 10% of daily energy, and 5% or less may bring extra benefits. It suggests at least 350 g of fruit and vegetables a day at ages 6 to 9.

The NHS suggests swapping cakes, biscuits and cereal bars for fruit bread or whole fruit, and keeping dried fruit to mealtimes for the sake of teeth.

Our opinion, based on the restriction research above: a small treat now and then, served without fuss, may work better than a total ban. For play ideas, see our active guide.

The short version

  • Fussiness is common and largely inherited. In 1 large study it fell from 27.6% of children at age 3 to 13.2% at age 6.
  • Calm, repeated tastes help. Expect 10 to 20 tries, and small steps.
  • Pressure, bribes and bans tend to backfire. You decide what, when and where. Your child decides how much.
  • Subject to Parliament, from September 2027 English school meals drop fruit juice and deep-fried food. Primary schools are limited to 1 sweet pudding a week.
  • Keep free sugars to 19 g a day at 4 to 6 and 24 g at 7 to 10, and juice to 150 ml.

When to ask your doctor or health visitor for help

  • Your child is losing weight or not growing as expected. Clothes or shoes that no longer need a bigger size can be a sign, says AboutKidsHealth from Canada's SickKids hospital.
  • The list of foods your child eats keeps shrinking, or they suddenly refuse foods they used to eat.
  • Your child panics at new foods, or avoids food out of fear of choking or vomiting.
  • Meals take very long, your child feels full after a few mouthfuls, or avoids parties because of food. The UK charity Beat lists these among the signs of avoidant restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID).
  • For scale, a 2015 Swiss study of 1,444 children aged 8 to 13 found that 3.2% reported features of ARFID, though self-report may overstate it. Ask your general practitioner (GP) or school nurse. In the UK you can also contact the Beat helpline.
  • If your child refuses all food and most drinks, or seems very weak, get advice the same day from your GP or, in the UK, call NHS 111. If your child is choking and cannot breathe, cough or cry, call your emergency number (999 in the UK, 112 in Europe, 911 in the US). See our choking guide.

Sensory play by age, birth to 5: simple ideas from things you already have, with a free printable

Your feed is full of colour-sorted trays and pricey sensory toys, while your baby happily chews a wooden spoon. If that leaves you wondering whether you are doing enough, you are probably doing more than you think. Here are simple ideas for every age from birth to 5, the safety rules that matter, and a 1-page chart to print.

Pinterest searches for sensory play rose 1,070%. You already own the kit

Pinterest's Parenting Trend Report 2026, published on 24 February 2026, showed searches for "sensory play ideas" up 1,070% on the year before. On Mumsnet, parents ask which baby classes were actually worth the money. A lot of parents feel they should be doing more.

Sensory play simply means letting your child use their senses: touch, sight, sound, smell and taste, plus balance and movement. A cold flannel counts. So does a pan lid, a puddle or a cut lemon.

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) agrees that you do not need special kit. Its report on play, reaffirmed in January 2025, says play is helped by "many inexpensive toys" and "simple available household objects". What matters most, it says, is your presence and attention.

Why squishing a banana counts as learning

The AAP says play "enhances brain structure and function and promotes executive function", the skills behind planning, focusing and remembering. Sensory play touches 4 areas at once.

  • Senses. Each new texture, sound and smell gives your child something to notice and compare.
  • Movement. Reaching, crawling, pouring and climbing build strength and coordination.
  • Language. Say "cold", "sticky" or "bumpy" as their hand touches something, and the word arrives with the feeling. The AAP links play with a caregiver to language skills.
  • Calm. The AAP says shared joy during play helps regulate the body's stress response.

3 hours of play a day for toddlers sounds like a lot. It is easier than it looks

WHO rates these as strong recommendations based on very low certainty evidence. Use them as a guide for a typical day, and a rainy day indoors is fine. For screens, see our screens guide.

0 to 6 months: your face is the best toy in the house

  • Faces and voices. Talk, sing and pull faces close up. Pause so your baby can "answer".
  • Tummy time. On your chest while you are wide awake, or on a floor blanket, a few minutes at a time, building towards 30 minutes a day.
  • Textures. A soft towel, a cool flannel, a wooden spoon, your woolly jumper. Name each texture.
  • Sounds. A plastic bottle with a little dried rice inside, lid glued and taped shut. You hold it.
  • Light and movement. Lie under a tree and watch the leaves.

6 to 12 months: everything goes in the mouth, so plan for it

  • Food play at meals. Squished banana, cooked pasta and yoghurt on the tray. See our first foods guide.
  • Treasure basket. A wooden spoon, a metal bowl, a 1-piece silicone spatula, a scarf and a large cup.
  • Kitchen drums. A pan and a wooden spoon.
  • Water tray. A shallow tray of water and 2 cups, with you right there. Empty it straight after.
  • Squish bag. Blobs of non-toxic paint in a strong freezer bag, sealed and taped flat to the tray.

1 to 2 years: pour, scoop, tip out, repeat

  • Pouring station. A washing-up bowl of water, cups, a funnel and a sieve, with you within arm's reach. Empty it straight after.
  • Posting. Post large jar lids through a slot in a cardboard box.
  • Barefoot walks. Grass, sand, a cold patio, puddles in wellies.
  • Frozen rescue. Freeze a large toy in a tub. Free it with warm water and a spoon.
  • Big box. A large cardboard box to climb in, push and hide in.

2 to 3 years: messy play and pretend play take off

  • Washing day. Soapy water to wash dolls, toy cars or plastic plates.
  • Painting with water. A big brush and a small bucket of water on a fence or wall.
  • Digging. Soil or sand with spoons. Plant seeds together, with you holding the packet, and check them daily. Wash hands afterwards.
  • Feely bag. Large objects in a pillowcase to guess by touch.
  • Cushion course. Climb over, crawl under, jump off the bottom step.

3 to 5 years: sorting, counting, guessing and words

  • Sorting tray. Dried pasta shapes sorted into a muffin tin, then counted.
  • Smell game. Lemon, a cinnamon stick and mint in cups. Eyes closed, guess the smell.
  • Baking. Measuring, stirring and kneading are maths and sensory play at once. No tasting raw dough, as raw flour can carry harmful bacteria.
  • Nature walk. Collect leaves, bark and stones, then sort rough from smooth.
  • Sound walk. Stand still for 1 minute and count every sound.

6 safety rules that matter more than any perfect set-up

  1. Stay with them, every time. Small children can quickly put things in their mouth, nose or ears.
  2. Use the toilet roll test for under 3s. The Canadian Paediatric Society says that if an object fits inside a toilet paper roll, it is a choking hazard. Dried beans, rice, pasta, buttons and coins all fail. Keep them for over 3s, with you, away from younger siblings. See our choking guide.
  3. Leave water beads out completely. The UK Office for Product Safety and Standards (OPSS) says swallowed beads can grow to over 400 times their size, may need surgery and do not show on X-ray. Its alert of 19 September 2024 says to keep them away from under 5s and out of homes with young children, even for older children. The US Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) estimates 6,300 emergency department visits for swallowed water beads from 2017 to 2022, and reports the death of a 10-month-old girl in 2023. Cooked pasta gives a similar squishy feel.
  4. Treat any water as a drowning risk. The Child Accident Prevention Trust (CAPT) says most home drownings of babies and small children happen in a few centimetres of water, silently. Stay within arm's reach and empty trays, buckets and paddling pools straight after.
  5. Think about food and allergies. The National Health Service (NHS) advises introducing foods that can trigger allergies 1 at a time, so you can spot a reaction. Offer a new food at a meal before using it in play, and keep known allergens out. Do not let children eat homemade salt dough. A poison centre at Vanderbilt University Medical Center found that 1 tablespoon of some recipes could put a 10 kg child in the toxic range for salt.
  6. Check for button batteries and magnets. Light-up toys, musical cards and remote controls hide them. OPSS says a swallowed button battery can burn a hole in the food pipe, and Health Canada says in as little as 2 hours. Swallowed magnets can pull together inside the gut and cut off its blood supply. See our home safety guide.

The short version

  • A kitchen, a garden and your attention are enough. Expensive kit is optional.
  • WHO: 30 minutes of tummy time a day for babies not yet mobile, and 180 minutes of play a day from age 1.
  • Always supervise. Under 3, nothing that fits through a toilet roll tube. No water beads.
  • Empty water straight away, try foods at meals first, and lock away button batteries and magnets.

When to talk to your doctor or health visitor

  • You think your child has swallowed a button battery or magnets. OPSS says go straight to accident and emergency (A&E), even with no symptoms. Do not give food or drink, or try to make them sick.
  • You think your child has swallowed water beads. OPSS says seek medical help immediately. Vomiting or tummy pain can start hours, or even a day or more, later.
  • Choking that does not clear, or signs the NHS treats as an emergency, such as difficulty breathing or blue, grey or pale skin, lips or tongue. Call 999 in the UK, 112 across Europe or 911 in the US. Our choking guide shows the first aid.
  • Breathing problems or a swollen throat or tongue after touching or tasting a food. The NHS says this can be anaphylaxis, a medical emergency.

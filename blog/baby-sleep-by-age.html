Baby sleep by age, birth to 5: what is normal, and why "sleeping through" means 6 hours

It is 3am, you are feeding again, and somewhere online a schedule says your baby should sleep 12 hours by now. You are probably doing nothing wrong. Here are the official sleep ranges from 3 health bodies, the real numbers on night waking, and what research says about regressions, wake windows, solids and sleep training.

WHO says 14 to 17 hours, the NHS says around 18: the official ranges side by side

The World Health Organization (WHO), the UK National Health Service (NHS) and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) each publish guidance. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) endorses the AASM figures. All totals are per 24 hours and include naps.

Look at how wide each range is. A 6-month-old on 12 hours and another on 16 hours are both inside WHO and US guidance. The NHS figures sit towards the top of the other 2, and above WHO for newborns. For more detail, see our baby sleep guide and toddler sleep guide.

38% of 6-month-olds do not sleep 6 hours in a row

When parents say "sleeping through", they often picture 7pm to 7am. Researchers usually mean 6 hours in a row, sometimes 8. By that definition, a baby who sleeps from 7pm to 1am has slept through.

A Canadian study led by Marie-Hélène Pennestri, published in the journal Pediatrics in 2018, asked mothers about their babies' sleep.

  • At 6 months, 38% of babies had not yet slept 6 hours in a row, and 57% had not slept 8.
  • At 12 months, 28% had not slept 6 hours in a row, and 43% had not slept 8.
  • Babies who did not sleep through showed no difference in development, and mothers' mood was not linked to night sleep length.

Breastfed babies were more likely to wake at least once a night. That is normal, and no reason to stop breastfeeding. The researchers also noted that some mothers felt tense and low when they tried to make their baby sleep through. If a schedule is making you miserable, you are allowed to put it down.

The "4-month regression": app data found no dip at 4 months

Economist Emily Oster summarised the research on her site ParentData. A 2016 study of baby app data found that night sleep got longer across the 1st year, with no dip at 4 months. A 2018 review found that parents reported more waking between 3 and 6 months, but wearable trackers did not pick it up. Night sleep also varied by about 2 hours from day to day. Oster calls regressions a "poorly branded term for normal sleep variability".

Even the Sleep Foundation, a US site that writes about the regression at length, says not all babies have it. Oster argues that disruptions can last longer when parents change how they respond at night, because babies form habits quickly. If you can, keep the bedtime steps the same for a week or 2.

Wake windows: search interest up 3,700%, and no studies behind them

Wake windows are charts that say how long a baby "should" stay awake between sleeps at each age. Search interest has grown by about 3,700% in recent years, according to the trend tool Exploding Topics.

The evidence is thin. In a 2024 article in the parenting magazine Romper, a sleep consultant agreed that no studies have looked at wake windows. Paediatric sleep specialist Dr Craig Canapari said the term is "not really used in the sleep medicine field". He added that following them is unlikely to be harmful.

The bigger risk is guilt. The timings come from other babies. If yours is ready for bed early, or happy to stay up longer, nothing has gone wrong. Watch your own baby's tired signs first. A simple record also beats any chart: Hoppo's sleep log shows what your child actually does over a week or 2.

The last nap can disappear anywhere from before 3 to around 4

Newborns sleep in short stretches across the day and night. By 6 to 12 months, the NHS says most sleep happens at night. Official bodies do not set fixed nap counts for each age, and the last nap is where many parents feel lost. WHO says sleep at 3 to 4 years "may include a nap". The NHS says some young children still need a nap. On Mumsnet, parents describe children dropping it anywhere from before 3 to age 4.

Many parents describe a child who suddenly wakes at 5am again, or melts down by bedtime. Tips in a single Mumsnet thread pulled in different directions: an earlier bedtime, a short capped nap, or quiet time with books and the curtains closed. All 3 are reasonable, so give 1 about a week.

Solids and sleep: a trial of 1,303 babies found about 16 extra minutes

Parents often hear that starting solids will help a baby sleep through. The UK EAT study, led by Michael Perkin and published in JAMA Pediatrics in 2018, tested this with 1,303 breastfed babies. Those who started solids from 3 months slept 16.6 minutes longer per night at the peak, around 6 months. Night waking fell from just over 2 times a night to 1.74.

That is a real effect, but small. The UK Food Standards Agency said the findings had limitations and asked parents to keep to existing advice. WHO and the AAP recommend breast milk only for about the first 6 months, and the NHS advises starting solids at around 6 months, when a baby can sit up with a steady head, bring food to their mouth and swallow it. The NHS lists waking in the night more than usual as normal baby behaviour that does not by itself mean a baby is ready. See our first foods guide.

Sleep training: what a 5-year follow-up found, and what critics say

Here is the evidence, with no course to sell. In Melbourne, Australia, Anna Price and colleagues ran a trial with 326 babies who had sleep problems at 7 months. Some families had 1 to 3 nurse sessions offering controlled comforting or "camping out", known in the UK as gradual retreat. At age 5 to 6, the study, published in Pediatrics in 2012, found no differences in emotional or behaviour problems, stress hormone levels, closeness, attachment or parents' mental health.

In Adelaide, Michael Gradisar and colleagues (Pediatrics, 2016) tested graduated extinction, where parents wait gradually longer before checking, and bedtime fading, a temporarily later bedtime matched to when the baby actually falls asleep. With 43 babies, both groups fell asleep in about 5 to 10 minutes instead of 20, and the graduated extinction group also woke less often. Stress hormone levels stayed normal, and attachment checked 12 months later showed no harm. The sample was small.

Critics, including a German commentary on the Price trial, point out that finding no difference does not prove there is no harm. That is fair.

What Hoppo suggests: if your family's sleep works for you, there is nothing to fix. If it does not, both gentle and more structured methods have evidence, and you can choose what fits your child and your family. A health visitor or nurse can help you make a plan.

Safe sleep for babies, every sleep

  • On their back for every sleep, day and night.
  • A clear cot with a firm, flat, waterproof mattress. No pillows, duvets, bumpers, loose blankets, soft toys, or pods and nests that hold your baby in place.
  • A smoke-free home, and a baby who is not too hot.
  • In the same room as you for at least the first 6 months, day and night. The NHS, the Lullaby Trust and the AAP all advise this.
  • Never fall asleep with your baby on a sofa or armchair. The Lullaby Trust says this can raise the risk of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) by up to 50 times.
  • No weighted swaddles, sleeping bags or other weighted products. The AAP says weighted products on or near a baby are not safe.

Countries differ on sharing a bed. In a Lullaby Trust survey of over 3,400 UK parents, 9 in 10 co-slept with their baby, but only 9% of them had planned to before the birth. So the NHS and the Lullaby Trust give safer co-sleeping advice, including never co-sleeping if anyone in the bed has been drinking alcohol, smokes, or has taken recreational drugs or medicine that makes them drowsy, or if your baby was born before 37 weeks or weighed under 2.5 kg at birth. In the US, the AAP "cannot support bed-sharing under any circumstances".

Our view: a cot in your room is the safest place. If you think you might fall asleep with your baby, prepare your bed safely in advance, because a sofa or armchair is far riskier. The full checklist is in our safe sleep guide.

The 3am version

  • Official sleep totals are wide ranges, and the bodies differ.
  • "Sleeping through" means about 6 hours. At 6 months, 38% of babies do not do it yet.
  • Data show no universal 4-month regression, and no studies support fixed wake windows.
  • Early solids added about 16 minutes of sleep. Advice still says around 6 months.
  • In 1 trial, sleep training showed no harm at age 5 to 6. Not sleep training is equally valid.
  • Back to sleep, clear cot, same room for 6 months, never asleep together on a sofa.

When to talk to your doctor or health visitor

  • Your child snores loudly with pauses in breathing, or gasps in their sleep. NHS Healthier Together advises seeing your GP about possible sleep apnoea.
  • Your child has difficulty breathing, or has blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin, lips or tongue. Call your emergency number (999 in the UK, 112 across Europe, 911 in the US) or go to the nearest emergency department, as the NHS advises.
  • Night waking comes with a fever or other signs of illness. Our fever guide explains when to get help.
  • You feel low or unable to cope. The NHS advises speaking to your GP, midwife or health visitor, even with only some of the signs. If you feel you cannot keep yourself or your baby safe, call your emergency number or go to the nearest emergency department now. See our low mood guide.
  • Sleep has been hard on your family for weeks and nothing helps.

Falling out or bullying? In their 4th year of school, 15% of children report bullying about weekly. 3 tests tell them apart

Your child comes home in tears because a best friend said "you are not my friend any more". By Friday they are playing together again. Another child hears the same words every day from the same group, and starts to dread the school gate. Both hurt. Only 1 of them is bullying. Here are the 3 tests experts use, the real numbers, what to say and how to work with the school.

115 replies on 1 thread: the question parents keep asking

On the Mumsnet bullying board, read on 1 November 2026, a thread from 21 July 2026 drew 115 replies. A teacher had described the behaviour behind it as normal for Year 1 and Year 2. The parent did not agree.

Other threads from July 2026 are similar. A mother found out her almost 7 year old had spent break times alone, because her friendship group would not let her join in. A parent of a shy Year 5 girl, whose 2 friends had started whispering and leaving her out, asked whether to change schools.

The 3 tests: repeated, on purpose, and a power imbalance

The UK Anti-Bullying Alliance defines bullying as the repetitive, intentional hurting of 1 person or group by another person or group, where the relationship involves an imbalance of power. It can happen face to face or online.

The US government site stopbullying.gov, reviewed on 7 October 2024, describes aggressive behaviour with a real or perceived power imbalance, which happens more than once or could happen again. The power can come from strength, popularity or embarrassing information. The NSPCC and the Department for Education (DfE) in England, in its 2017 advice for schools, use the same ideas.

So the 3 tests are simple. Check whether it happens again and again. Check whether it is meant to hurt. Check whether 1 side is stronger, more popular or in a group, so that the other child cannot easily make it stop.

SignA typical fall-outMore likely bullying
How oftenA single row, or a bad few daysThe same thing again and again, often for weeks
Who is upsetBoth children feel hurt or cross1 child is upset, the other seems to enjoy it or not care

This table is Hoppo's summary of the definitions, not an official tool. A fall-out can turn into bullying if 1 child gathers a group and keeps it going. The Scottish Government's Parent Club site warns about "bullying disguised as friendship", where a so-called friend cuts your child off from other friends, tells them what to wear or takes their money or things.

How common bullying is in the primary years

Every survey asks the question in a different way, so the numbers below cannot be compared directly. Read them as a range.

  • 4th year of school, international: in the Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) 2023, children in their 4th year of school, average age at least 9.5, reported on the past year. 15% were bullied about weekly, 31% about monthly and 53% never or almost never.
  • Age 11, 13 and 15, Europe: the Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC) study surveyed over 279,000 young people in 44 countries in 2021 and 2022. WHO Europe reported on 27 March 2024 that about 11% were bullied at school and 15% online. Online bullying rose between 2018 and 2022.
  • Slovenia: in the HBSC 2022 report from the National Institute of Public Health (NIJZ), 31.5% of 11 year olds said they had been bullied at school at least once in the past few months. It is a looser measure.
  • Age 15: in the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2022, 8.3% of students across the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) were frequently bullied, and 10.8% in Scotland, the Scottish Government reported on 5 December 2023.
  • UK helpline signal: the NSPCC says bullying was the main concern in 6,617 Childline counselling sessions in 2024 to 2025. That shows demand, not prevalence.

In TIMSS, children bullied about weekly scored 461 in maths on average, against 520 for those rarely or never bullied. That is a link, not proof of cause.

Why it matters, and what the long studies can and cannot show

A British study followed 7,771 children born in 1 week in 1958. Parents reported bullying at age 7 and 11. 28% were bullied occasionally and 15% frequently. At age 50, those bullied often had poorer mental and physical health and weaker social ties, even after allowing for childhood IQ, behaviour and family background. The lead author, Professor Louise Arseneault, said bullying should not be seen as an inevitable part of growing up.

A US study followed 1,420 children from age 9 to 26. Those bullied had higher rates of anxiety disorders as adults. Children who were both bullied and bullied others had the highest risks. Both studies show strong links, not final proof.

A 2017 review in the World Journal of Psychiatry combined 165 studies. It found the evidence was strongest for a causal link between being bullied and later mental health problems, such as depression, anxiety and suicidal thoughts. For other problems, the evidence was too weak to say.

Schools can make a difference. A 2021 Campbell review of 100 evaluations found school anti-bullying programmes cut bullying by about 18% to 19% and being bullied by about 15% to 16%. The authors called the effects modest. In Finland, a trial of the KiVa programme in 78 schools, with 8,237 pupils in grades 4 to 6, cut bullying and victimisation within 9 months.

What to say to your child, and what to avoid

The NSPCC suggests choosing a time when you can give your full attention. Some children talk more easily in the car. Stay calm, even if you feel upset. Parent Club suggests short, frequent chats rather than 1 long talk.

  • To open up: Hoppo suggests an open, everyday question, such as "Who did you play with at lunch today?", rather than "Is anyone bullying you?"
  • To listen: "Can you tell me more?" and "What was that like for you?", as the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) suggests.
  • To reassure: "Thank you for telling me. It is not your fault. We will sort it out together."
  • To check the 3 tests: "Has this happened before? Does it stop when you ask? Is it 1 child or a group?"
  • To practise: the AAP describes "stop, walk, talk". Say a firm "Enough" or "Not cool", walk away calmly, then tell a trusted adult. Rehearse it at home.
  • About telling: "Telling a grown-up is not telling tales. It takes courage." The AAP makes the same point.

In Hoppo's view, "just ignore it" on its own and "hit them back" tend not to help. The AAP says calling the other child's parents is not the first step.

For a plain fall-out, Parent Club advises listening, saying that it is normal to fall out with friends sometimes, and encouraging your child to solve the problem. Step in only if there is fighting. The NSPCC and the AAP both suggest clubs and activities outside school to build confidence and friendships.

If your child is the one bullying, the NSPCC suggests explaining why it is not acceptable, helping them think about the other child's feelings, setting clear expectations and keeping watch.

Step by step with the school

In England, the DfE says the Education and Inspections Act 2006 requires maintained schools to have measures to prevent all forms of bullying. In Slovenia, a ministry circular of 4 March 2022 asked schools for zero tolerance of peer violence and to follow the national protocol of the National Education Institute (ZRSŠ) in every case. The steps below come mainly from the NSPCC.

  1. Keep a log. Write down the date, time, place, who was involved and what was said or done. Keep photos of injuries or damaged things, and screenshots of messages.
  2. Read the policies. Ask for the anti-bullying, behaviour and complaints policies.
  3. Start with the class teacher. Ask for a meeting. Bring your notes and examples of how it affects your child. You can bring someone with you.
  4. Agree a plan and a review date. Ask what the school will do and who will watch at break times. In Hoppo's view, review after about 2 weeks.
  5. Ask for a key person. Your child should know which adult to go to.
  6. Escalate in writing if it continues. The NSPCC lists the order: a second meeting, a letter to the headteacher under the complaints policy, a letter to the chair of governors, then the local authority. The DfE can give further advice.

A calm opening line: "I am not sure yet whether this is a fall-out or bullying. Can we look at it together and agree what happens next?" If the bullying involves ongoing harassment or intimidation, or is a hate crime such as racism or homophobia, tell the school straight away. The NSPCC says the school may inform the police.

Online bullying starts earlier than many parents expect

Ofcom's report of 7 May 2025 found that 33% of UK children aged 6 to 7 use WhatsApp, and 40% of under-13s have a social media profile. Among 8 and 9 year olds who go online, 20% said they had experienced someone being nasty or hurtful to them through communication technology, such as messages or apps.

Stopbullying.gov says online bullying can reach a child at any hour, stays public unless reported and removed, and is harder for adults to see. The NSPCC says young people tell Childline that bullying is taking place in group chats.

  • Do not take the device away first. The NSPCC says this is unlikely to solve things, and your child may go online elsewhere or in secret.
  • Save the evidence with screenshots.
  • Block and report on the platform where it happened.
  • Tell the school if the children involved are classmates.
  • If a nude or sexual image of a child is shared, the NSPCC points to the Childline and Internet Watch Foundation Report Remove tool.

The short version

  • Fall-outs are normal. Bullying is repeated, meant to hurt, and involves a power imbalance.
  • In their 4th year of school, about 15% of children say they are bullied about weekly.
  • Listen first, praise your child for telling you, and keep a written log.
  • Work with the class teacher, agree a plan and a review date, then escalate in writing if it continues.
  • Online bullying can start in primary school. Save evidence, block, report and keep the device.

When to ask your doctor or health visitor for help

  • Your child has tummy aches, headaches, poor sleep or nightmares for several weeks, or does not want to go to school (NSPCC, stopbullying.gov). Our worries guide explains what to look for.
  • Your child seems low, anxious or withdrawn most days.
  • Same day: your child talks about hurting themselves, not wanting to be alive, or running away. Speak to your GP the same day. In England, you can call NHS 111 and choose the mental health option.
  • Emergency: call 999 in the UK, 112 across Europe or 911 in the US if your child is injured, threatened with a weapon or in immediate danger, or after serious self-harm. Stopbullying.gov says to call after a crime or when someone is at immediate risk.
  • Where to get help: Childline for children on 0800 1111, free and 24 hours a day. The NSPCC Helpline for adults on 0808 800 5000. In Slovenia, TOM telefon for children and teenagers on 116 111, daily from 12:00 to 20:00. In the US, call or text 988 if someone is thinking about suicide.

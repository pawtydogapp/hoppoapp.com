Choosing baby formula: what a higher price does not buy, and whether toddler milk is needed

You stand in the baby aisle with a £6.99 tin in one hand and a £14.00 tin in the other, and the cheaper one feels like a risk to your baby. If you have ever paid more just to be safe, you are far from alone. Here is what the law puts in every tin, what the label extras mean, and why most children need no formula after their first birthday.

The short answer: 1 type of formula for year 1, then ordinary milk

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends breast milk only for the first 6 months, then breastfeeding alongside food up to 2 years or beyond. Many parents also use formula, fully or alongside breastfeeding, for health reasons, to share feeds, for work, or because it works for their family. All of those are good reasons. This guide is for anyone who buys formula, with no judgement.

The UK National Health Service (NHS) puts the rest simply:

  • First infant formula is suitable from birth. Unless a midwife, health visitor or doctor suggests otherwise, it is the only formula your baby needs.
  • From 12 months, formula milks are not needed. That includes follow-on, growing-up and toddler milks.

Why a £6.99 tin and a £14.00 tin feed your baby equally well

In the European Union (EU), every infant formula must meet 1 law: Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2016/127. Since 22 February 2020 it has set what goes into infant and follow-on formula, and how they are labelled. The UK kept the same rules as UK law after Brexit, and they still apply directly in Northern Ireland.

For the tin in your hand, that means:

  • Every ingredient must be shown to be suitable for babies from birth by generally accepted scientific data.
  • Every infant formula must contain docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), a fat found in breast milk. The cheapest tin has it too.
  • Nutrition and health claims are banned on infant formula. A tin may not claim to boost brains or immunity.

The NHS says it plainly: "All infant formulas will meet your baby's nutritional needs, regardless of brand or price."

If you live outside the EU and UK, your country will have its own formula rules. Your national food safety agency can tell you what they require.

So why do so many parents pay double?

The UK Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) studied the formula market and reported on 14 February 2025. It found that many parents first choose a brand "often in hospital immediately after birth", without impartial information. Many "actively choose a more expensive product, assuming this means better quality."

The numbers are large:

  • Just 3 firms supply more than 90% of UK formula, the CMA found.
  • Switching from a popular mid-priced product to a low-priced one could save around £300 over a baby's first year. Switching from a premium brand could save up to £500. Both CMA figures assume formula feeding only for 12 months.
  • In June 2025, the Food Foundation found the cheapest first infant formula at £6.99 for 800 g, at 2 discount supermarkets. The dearest it found cost £14.00 for the same size, more than twice as much.

These are UK prices, and your shelf will differ. The same legal standard applies across the EU and UK, so a higher price does not mean better nutrition.

On parenting forums, many parents swap brand names as if the brand were the answer. That instinct comes from love. You can let it go.

First, follow-on, growing-up: the tins you can skip

EU law says follow-on packs must look clearly different from infant formula. Even so, the CMA found manufacturers use similar branding across both.

TypeSuitable fromDoes your child need it?
First infant formulaBirthYes, if you formula feed. It covers the whole first year
Follow-on formula6 months, never earlierNo. NHS: you do not need to introduce it
Growing-up or toddler milk1 yearNo. NHS and AAP: not needed after 12 months

WHO and UNICEF class milks marketed for children up to 3 years, including follow-up formula and growing-up milks, as breast-milk substitutes. Read the name and the age on the front, not just the colour.

Toddler milk: more sugar and cost, with no extra benefit

Growing-up milks are sold as the next step after 12 months. The evidence finds extra cost and sugar, with no benefit for most children.

  • In 2024, the UK charity First Steps Nutrition Trust found that formula milks, including growing-up and toddler milks, were the top source of free sugars for 12 to 18-month-olds who drink them, at 49.8% of their free sugar intake.
  • For 14 of 16 products for 1 to 2-year-olds, just 350 ml went over the recommended free sugar limit.
  • Parents could spend about £14 more a month than on regular cows' milk.
  • The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) said in October 2023 that toddler drinks are "generally unnecessary and nutritionally incomplete", and typically cost more than cows' milk.
  • The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) concluded in 2013 that growing-up formula has no additional value over a balanced diet.

What to give instead depends a little on where you live. The NHS says whole or semi-skimmed cows' milk can be a main drink from 1 year. The AAP suggests whole cows' milk from 12 months. The NHS also allows unsweetened, calcium-fortified plant drinks such as soya or oat from 1 year, but no rice drinks under 5 because of arsenic. Breastfeeding can carry on as long as you both want.

Hoppo suggests milk or water as main drinks after the first birthday, alongside family food.

The 70°C rule and other safety basics

Powdered formula is not sterile, even in a sealed tin. Hot water kills the bacteria. These steps come from the NHS and WHO:

  1. Boil at least 1 litre of fresh tap water. Do not reuse water that has been boiled before.
  2. Let it cool for no more than 30 minutes, so it stays at 70°C or above. WHO says the same: no cooler than 70°C.
  3. Wash your hands and clean the surface. Use sterilised bottles and teats.
  4. Pour the water into the bottle first, while it is still hot, then add powder with the scoop from that tin, levelled off, exactly as the label says.
  5. Cool the closed bottle under cold running water. Test a few drops on your inner wrist. It should feel warm or cool, not hot.
  6. Throw away any milk left after a feed. WHO says to discard any feed not used within 2 hours.

Never add extra water to make a tin last longer. The NHS warns that too little powder may not give your baby enough nourishment, and too much can cause constipation or dehydration. If money is tight, switch to a cheaper first formula instead. Your health visitor or doctor can also tell you what support exists where you live.

Some specialist formulas, such as some anti-reflux milks, are made up differently. The NHS says to follow the instructions on the pack, or the advice of your health visitor or GP, for those.

A few more NHS rules: make feeds 1 at a time, never heat formula in a microwave, and add nothing such as sugar or cereal to the bottle. Bottled water is not usually recommended. If you use a preparation machine, check that its water comes out at 70°C or above.

Changing brands: what to ask before you swap

Swapping between standard first infant formulas to save money is fine, because all of them meet your baby's needs. Swapping to fix crying or spitting up is different. The NHS says there is no evidence that switching formula does any good or harm. Read our guides to crying and spitting up first.

If you suspect reflux or a milk allergy, talk to your health visitor or doctor before you change anything. A diagnosed cows' milk allergy needs a special formula prescribed by a doctor, and a brand swap will not fix it. The 30-second version

  • Every infant formula in the EU and UK must meet the same legal standard. The NHS says all meet your baby's needs, whatever the price.
  • Switching to a cheaper first formula could save around £300 to £500 in the first year, according to the CMA.
  • First infant formula covers the whole first year. Follow-on formula is optional.
  • After 12 months, toddler milk is not needed. Most are high in free sugars and cost more than cows' milk.
  • Make feeds with water at 70°C or above, follow the scoop rule exactly, and never water down formula.

When to talk to your doctor or health visitor

  • Your baby has fewer than around 6 wet nappies a day after the first few days, or you are worried about weight gain (NHS).
  • You think your baby may have a milk allergy (NHS).
  • Your baby is on a specialist or prescribed formula and you want to change it.
  • Call your emergency number if your baby's lips, mouth or tongue suddenly swell, they struggle to breathe, their skin turns blue, grey or pale, or they become limp, floppy or unresponsive (NHS).

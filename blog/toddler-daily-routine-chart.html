The toddler routine that bends without breaking: 3 sample days for ages 1 to 5, and a free printable chart

You want calmer mornings and easier bedtimes, but a minute-by-minute schedule makes you tired just looking at it. You do not need one. Here are 3 flexible sample days built on World Health Organization (WHO) guidance, simple tricks for the moments that usually end in tears, and a 1-page picture chart to print and tick off with your child.

Pinterest searches for routine charts rose 575%. Parents want a rhythm, not a timetable

Pinterest's Parenting Trend Report 2026, published on 24 February 2026, says searches for "daily routine chart for kids" rose 575%. Treat that number as a signal, not a study. Pinterest does not say which countries it covers, how it measured, or the exact time window for this term.

The wish behind it is easy to recognise. Many parents on Mumsnet describe the same days: the shoe battle at the door, the nap that suddenly vanished, the bedtime that drags on with tears. What they want is a shape to the day that survives a late night, a sick week or a visit from Grandma.

So here is the good news. The order of the day matters more than the clock. If breakfast is at 7.40am instead of 7.15am, nothing is lost, as long as breakfast still comes before getting dressed, every day.

Why doing things in the same order means fewer battles

Toddlers cannot read a clock, but they learn fast what comes next. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) puts it simply: routines and rules help children feel safe and know what to expect.

The Raising Children Network in Australia adds a practical point. A routine sets out who does what, when and in what order, and that helps children cooperate. When teeth always come before the story, there is less to argue about.

Sleep is where the evidence is strongest. A 2015 study in the journal Sleep surveyed 10,085 mothers of children aged 0 to 5, in 13 countries and regions. Children with a regular bedtime routine went to bed earlier, fell asleep faster, woke less at night and slept longer. The more nights a week the routine happened, the better the results.

2 honest limits. It was a survey, so it shows a link and cannot prove cause. It was also funded by a baby-products company. Still, the findings match what children's sleep services advise. For bedtime struggles in more depth, see our toddler sleep guide.

The 4 WHO numbers behind every sample day

In 2019 the WHO published guidelines for children under 5 that look at the whole 24 hours: sleep, movement and sitting still. They shape the sample days below.

180 minutes sounds like a lot. It counts everything: walking to the shop, climbing the sofa, dancing in the kitchen, pushing a toy pram. It does not need to happen in 1 block.

The WHO also encourages reading and storytelling with a caregiver when children are sitting. For ages 1 to 4 it asks for "regular sleep and wake-up times", which is a routine in all but name.

Advice on screens differs between countries. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) used to make an exception for video calls under 18 months and allow some high-quality programmes watched together from 18 months. In January 2026 it replaced that advice with a policy that moves away from fixed time limits and focuses on the quality of what children watch and on watching together. Our screens guide compares the advice. At 5, children move to the WHO guidance for ages 5 to 17, which asks for an average of 60 minutes a day of moderate to vigorous activity.

Sample day 1: around 1 year, when 2 naps start turning into 1

Most toddlers move from 2 naps to 1 somewhere between 12 and 18 months, according to Plunket in New Zealand and the Sydney Children's Hospitals Network. Expect some 2-nap days and some 1-nap days while it settles. All times are a guide. Shift the whole day earlier or later to fit your family.

Sample day 3: 3 to 5 years, nap or quiet time

By 3 the daytime nap is usually shrinking and then stops, says the Sydney Children's Hospitals Network, though some preschoolers still need it. Parents in a Mumsnet thread on dropping the nap describe early waking and tearful bedtimes once the last nap goes. A fix they suggest is an earlier bedtime while the child adjusts. Most meltdowns start between activities. 5 ways to soften the switch

  1. Warn twice. "5 more minutes, then bath." Then "1 more slide, then bath." Toddlers have little sense of time, so tie the warning to something they can see: 2 more turns, the end of the song.
  2. Offer 2 choices you are happy with. "Blue pyjamas or the ones with stars?" "Hop to the bath or tiptoe?" The step still happens, but your child gets a say in how.
  3. Sing the same song for the same job. A tidy-up song, a teeth song, a shoes song. After a week or so, the first line starts to do the work for you.
  4. Use "first, then". "First shoes, then we go and see the ducks." The Raising Children Network suggests the same pattern: "First clean teeth. Then story time with Dad."
  5. Give them a job. Carrying the towel to the bath or pressing the lift button gives your child a role in the change.

Some tears will still happen. That is normal at this age and does not mean the routine has failed. Our tantrums guide covers what to do in the moment.

Make the chart together, and your child will want to use it

The Raising Children Network suggests making an illustrated poster of the routine with your child and putting it where everyone can see it. A chart they helped to make feels like theirs.

  1. Print the chart below, or copy it onto a big sheet of paper.
  2. Let your child draw a small picture next to each step, or stick on photos of them doing it.
  3. Keep it short. For under 3s, 3 or 4 steps per part of the day are plenty. Cross out what you do not need.
  4. Hang it at your child's eye level, where the morning starts, such as the bedroom door or the fridge.
  5. Tick together. Let your child make the tick or add a sticker. Point to the next picture instead of repeating the instruction.
  6. Praise the effort. "You put your shoes on all by yourself" is enough.

Under 2s will not follow a chart on their own. For them, the chart is for you: a reminder to keep the same order so they can learn it.

Nursery days, weekends and sick days: keep 3 anchors and let the rest go

Real life will bend the day. Hoppo suggests keeping 3 anchors steady: a similar wake-up time, meals at roughly the same times, and the same bedtime steps in the same order. Everything else can move.

  • Nursery days. Ask the staff when your child naps and eats there, and match home days to it where you can. Make a short "nursery morning" strip of the chart. Tired, grumpy evenings are common in the first weeks. Our nursery guide has more on settling in.
  • Weekends. Try to keep wake-up and bedtime within about 1 hour of weekdays. A long lie-in pushes the nap and bedtime later, and Monday morning gets harder.
  • Holidays and visits. Pack the bedtime steps. The same book, song and sleeping bag in a new place tell your child what comes next.
  • Illness. Put the chart away. Sick children often need extra sleep and cuddles, and a bit more screen time on a sick day is fine. If your child is very drowsy, hard to wake or you are worried, get medical help straight away. When they feel better, bring the routine back 1 step at a time, starting with bedtime.
  • Big changes. A new baby, a house move or a nursery start can unsettle a routine for a while. The CDC suggests talking about a change when your child is calm, and saying whether it is for a short time or for good.

The short version

  • The order of the day matters more than the clock.
  • WHO: 11 to 14 hours of sleep in 24 hours at ages 1 and 2, and 10 to 13 hours at ages 3 and 4.
  • At least 180 minutes of activity a day, in short bursts, and no more than 1 hour strapped in at a time.
  • WHO: no sedentary screen time at age 1, and no more than 1 hour at ages 2 to 4.
  • Warn twice, offer 2 choices, sing the same song.
  • On messy days, keep 3 anchors: wake-up, mealtimes and bedtime steps.

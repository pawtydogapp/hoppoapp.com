Newborn visitors and grandparents: the 5 rules that matter, and kind words that keep the peace

You want your family to meet your baby. You also want to stop the next person from kissing her face. This guide gives you 5 simple rules based on health body advice, the facts behind them, and ready-made words that protect your baby without starting a family row.

107 replies on 1 thread: you are not alone in feeling torn

In September 2026, a Mumsnet thread drew 107 replies. The in-laws were upset that a week-old baby had not been brought round, and the parents did not want her passed from lap to lap.

The same worry appears on a European ask-the-doctor forum, with about 88 posts since 2023 on cold sores, chickenpox and visitors. One mother describes telling everyone that nobody but her and her partner may kiss the baby. Another writes that an uncle with a fresh cold sore kissed her baby on the head.

Both sides love the baby. What usually goes missing is a clear set of facts and a kind way to say them.

Why the first weeks deserve extra care, and why it gets easier

A newborn's immune system is still developing. Some protection passes from Mum to baby in the womb and through breast milk, according to the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP). Vaccines take time to build on that. In the UK, a baby's own whooping cough vaccines start at 8 weeks, says the National Health Service (NHS).

Most infections that visitors carry cause nothing worse than a sniffle. A few matter more in the first months.

The strictest weeks are short. As your baby grows and starts her own vaccines, you can relax the rules step by step. For a baby who already has a cold, see our colds guide.

Cold sores and newborns: rare, serious and easy to prevent

  • It is rare. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates neonatal herpes at about 10 in every 100,000 births worldwide. Babies can catch it during birth, or later from a kiss.
  • It can be serious. The NHS says kissing a baby while you have a cold sore can lead to neonatal herpes, which is very dangerous to newborn babies. Ireland's Health Service Executive (HSE) says it can even be fatal.
  • The rule is simple. Anyone with a cold sore, or the tingle of a new sore, should not kiss the baby at all. That includes parents.
  • If Mum has a cold sore, HSE advises washing hands before touching the baby and covering the sore before breastfeeding.
  • Signs to watch for. AAP says small skin blisters or sores, sometimes with a fever, can appear 2 to 12 days after contact.

If someone with a cold sore has already kissed your baby, try not to panic. Ring your GP, midwife or health visitor the same day for advice. If your baby has a fever, a blister-like rash or seems unwell, get urgent advice straight away, using the signs at the end of this guide. The NHS says newborns may be referred to hospital for advice or treatment.

The 5 rules, and what health bodies say

  1. Wash hands before holding the baby. HSE says it is fine to ask every visitor to do this. AAP agrees that handwashing helps prevent infection.
  2. No kisses on the face. HSE and AAP both say no kisses from anyone with a cold sore. We suggest everyone skips kisses on the face and hands in the first weeks, because babies put their hands in their mouths.
  3. Stay away when ill. HSE asks anyone with a cough, cold, tummy bug or other viral infection to wait until they feel better. AAP says caregivers should return only once they are well.
  4. Check vaccines. See the next section.
  5. Keep visits short and spread out. HSE suggests spreading visitors over the days or weeks after the birth, in small groups.

The vaccines that protect a baby before she can have her own

When a mother is vaccinated in pregnancy, her antibodies pass to the baby through the placenta (NHS). Advice for grandparents and other close family differs between countries, so ask your GP, pharmacist or midwife what is offered where you live.

If a grandparent cannot get a booster where you live, the other 4 rules still help a lot.

How long should a visit last? Rest is part of recovery

No health body sets a time limit. We suggest about 1 hour in the first 2 weeks, ideally between feeds. A guest who stays all afternoon often means a Mum who cannot feed, nap or shower in peace.

After a caesarean, the NHS says you may need to take things easy for several weeks, and may not drive for 6 weeks or so. After any birth, the NHS advises resting when your baby sleeps, even if chores are waiting. HSE says you need time to rest and to get to know your baby.

Visitors who help beat visitors who only hold the baby

The NHS suggests asking a relative to look after the baby while you nap. HSE suggests asking for specific help, such as collecting nappies or emptying the dishwasher. A grandparent with a job feels useful. Ideas:

  • Bring a meal, or fill the freezer.
  • Put on a wash and hang it out.
  • Hold the baby while you shower or sleep for an hour.
  • Take an older child to the park, or do the shopping.

Kind but firm words for grandparents and in-laws

It often goes better when each partner talks to their own parents. Say it early, before the birth if you can. If your midwife gave you the advice, say so. Then offer something warm in return.

  • Kisses: "We are asking everyone to skip kisses on her face until she is a bit older. Cuddles are very welcome."
  • Feeling ill: "If you have any cough, cold or tummy bug, please wait until you are better. We would love to see you as soon as you are well."
  • Passing her round: "She gets overwhelmed in a crowd. Let us do 2 visitors at a time, and everyone gets a proper cuddle."
  • "We raised 3 and they were fine": "You did a brilliant job. The advice has changed a bit, and it would mean a lot if you followed it."
  • Coming round to yours: "We are staying home while I recover. Could you come to us for an hour on Sunday?"
  • Overstaying: "Thank you for coming. She needs a feed and I need a nap, so let us say goodbye for today."

A message to send to family

Hi everyone. We cannot wait for you to meet our baby. To keep a tiny new person safe in the first weeks, we are asking all visitors to:

  • Wash your hands when you arrive, before you hold the baby.
  • Skip kisses on the baby's face and hands.
  • Stay home if you have a cough, cold, tummy bug or cold sore, and come once you are well.
  • Check with your GP or pharmacist whether your whooping cough and flu vaccines are up to date.
  • Keep visits to about 1 hour, and text before you come.

Bringing food or doing a small job is the best present we could get. Thank you for helping us look after her.

When caution starts to feel like too much

Some parents describe washing and disinfecting far more than anyone asked. If worry about germs fills your day or stops you sleeping, talk to your health visitor or GP. Anxiety after a birth is common, and help works. Our low mood guide explains where to start.

The short version

  • Clean hands, no kisses on the face, and no visitors who are ill. These 3 everyday rules go a long way.
  • Nobody with a cold sore kisses the baby. Neonatal herpes is rare, serious and preventable.
  • Vaccines in pregnancy protect the baby. Advice on family boosters differs by country, so ask locally.
  • Short, spread-out visits from people who help leave room for rest.
  • Agree the rules with your partner, then say them early and kindly.

When to talk to your doctor or health visitor

  • Your baby is under 3 months and has a temperature of 38°C or higher. This is urgent. The NHS says to get urgent advice from NHS 111, and AAP says to call your child's doctor right away.
  • A seriously ill baby does not always have a raised temperature. Slovenia's National Institute of Public Health (NIJZ) advises seeing a doctor without delay if a baby seems seriously ill, drinks too little or is unusually sleepy.
  • Your newborn develops a blister-like rash, sores or a fever, especially after contact with someone who had a cold sore (AAP).
  • Call your emergency number (999 in the UK, 112 across Europe, 911 in the US) if your baby has difficulty breathing, pauses in breathing, blue, grey or blotchy skin, a rash that does not fade under a pressed glass, or is drowsy and hard to wake (NHS, NIJZ).

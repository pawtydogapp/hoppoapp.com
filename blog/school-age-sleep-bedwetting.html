9 to 12 hours a night, and 1 in 20 still wet the bed at 10: sleep from 5 to 10, by the evidence

School brings early alarms, homework, first phones and, for some families, wet sheets most mornings. This guide sets out how much sleep health bodies say children need, what a bedtime routine and screen cut-off can do, how to handle night fears, and why bedwetting is common, is not the child's fault and can be treated.

32 replies and 1 worn-down parent: what families ask after age 5

In May 2026, a Mumsnet parent described 2 children who wet the bed, a 7 year old with dry spells that come and go and a 5 year old who has never been dry. An alarm had not woken the older child in time. The 7 year old had started to avoid sleepovers. Of the 32 replies, some said an alarm worked within weeks, others only with an adult sleeping nearby.

Fear of the dark is the other big theme. In December 2025, People magazine reported a Reddit post about a nearly 9 year old who would not fall asleep without a parent in the room.

Screens add a newer layer. On 21 May 2026, the UK media regulator Ofcom reported that smartphone ownership rises from 56% of 10 year olds to 83% of 11 year olds. Treat this as a signal of change, not a study of sleep.

For a shorter checklist, see our sleep guide for school-age children and our bedwetting guide.

How many hours: what the AASM, CDC, NHS and NIJZ say

In 2016, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) published the most widely used figures. A panel of 13 sleep experts reviewed 864 scientific articles. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) endorsed the result.

SourceAgeRecommended sleep

The main NHS website page on children's sleep could not be reached when we checked, so the NHS row uses an NHS trust.

The AASM says that regularly sleeping less than the recommended amount is linked with attention, behaviour and learning problems. It is also linked with a higher risk of accidents, obesity and depression.

In practice, work back from the wake-up time. A 7 year old who gets up at 7am and aims for 10.5 hours, the middle of the AASM range, should be asleep by about 8.30pm.

Many children do not reach the range. For World Sleep Day in March 2024, RTV Slovenija reported figures showing that only 38.7% of Slovenian 11 and 13 year olds sleep the recommended amount on school nights.

Bedtime routines: why the same 30 minutes every night helps

A widely cited study on routines is from 2015. It surveyed 10,085 mothers in 13 countries and regions about children aged 0 to 5. Preschoolers with a routine every night slept about 10.2 hours at night, against about 9.1 hours for those who never had one.

The small print: it covered children under 6, used mothers' reports, shows a link rather than proof, and was sponsored by Johnson and Johnson.

Here is a routine you can adapt, based on AAP, CDC and NHS trust advice:

  1. Fix the wake-up time first. The AAP advises the same waking time on weekdays and weekends. Somerset NHS Foundation Trust suggests no more than 1 hour of difference.
  2. Start about 30 minutes before lights out. This is Hoppo's suggestion. Bath, pyjamas, teeth and toilet, in the same order each night.
  3. Dim the house. Somerset NHS Foundation Trust suggests lowering the lights and closing the curtains about 1 hour before bed.
  4. Keep the room cool and dark. The same trust suggests about 18°C. A dim red or orange night light is fine for a child who is afraid of the dark.
  5. Skip caffeine after lunch. The CDC advises avoiding caffeine in the afternoon and evening.
  6. End with 10 quiet minutes together. A chapter of a book or a chat about the day. Our reading guide has ideas that suit this age.
  7. Use the same last words. For example: "Story is finished. Lights out now. I love you, see you in the morning."

Screens before bed: 30 to 60 minutes, and phones out of the bedroom

The AAP, in advice updated on 7 August 2026, says to turn off all screens at least 1 hour before bedtime and to keep screens out of children's bedrooms. The CDC says to put screens away at least 30 minutes before bed.

The main evidence is a 2016 review of 20 studies with 125,198 children and teenagers aged 6 to 19. Using a device at bedtime was linked with about 2 times the odds of not sleeping enough (odds ratio 2.17). Just having a device in the bedroom, without using it, was also linked with too little sleep (odds ratio 1.79).

2 honest limits. The average age was 14.5, so younger children were a small part of the data. The studies observed families, so they show a link, not proof that devices cause poor sleep.

NIJZ advises avoiding interactive content before bed, because children lose track of time. A simple house rule is to charge every phone and tablet in the kitchen overnight, including the adults' phones. For limits during the day, see our screens guide for school-age children.

Night fears and nightmares: common, and usually a passing phase

A 2001 Dutch study interviewed 176 children aged 4 to 12, with their parents. 73.3% of the children reported night-time fears. Fears were more frequent at 7 to 9 than at 4 to 6. Parents tended to underestimate how often their children felt afraid. Nearly 80% of the children linked their fear to frightening things they had seen or heard.

The NHS separates 2 night-time events.

  • Nightmares are bad dreams a child wakes from and can remember.
  • Night terrors are most common between 3 and 8, says the NHS. The child may scream with open eyes but is still asleep. The NHS advises staying calm, not trying to wake them and not holding them down unless they could hurt themselves.

Words to try with a frightened child: "Your body feels scared, and you are safe. I am just downstairs. I will check on you in 5 minutes." Then come back when you said you would, and stretch the gap over time.

Replies to the Reddit post suggested small, planned steps, such as sitting together in a dimmer room for 5 minutes while telling jokes, and building up slowly. In our opinion, this beats long talks about monsters. If worries spill into the daytime, our worries guide explains next steps.

Bedwetting: how common it really is at 5, 7 and 10

Bedwetting, or nocturnal enuresis, means wetting during sleep. ERIC, the Children's Bowel and Bladder Charity, says it affects around half a million children aged 5 to 16 in the UK.

5About 1 in 5Sydney Children's Hospitals Network, May 2026. The American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry (AACAP) also says about 20%.
7About 1 in 10Sydney Children's Hospitals Network and AACAP. ERIC's undated night-time wetting factsheet says about 1 in 15.
7.515.5% wet at all, 2.6% at least twice a weekUK study of 8,151 children in Bristol, 2005
10About 1 in 20Sydney Children's Hospitals Network

The figures differ because some sources count any wet night and others only wetting at least twice a week. In the Bristol study, most of the 15.5% wet once a week or less.

ERIC describes 3 systems that need to mature.

  • A night-time hormone. Some children do not yet make enough vasopressin, the hormone that slows urine production at night. They often wet large amounts early in the night.
  • Bladder size. Some bladders do not yet stretch enough to hold the night's urine. These children may wet more than once a night.
  • Waking up. Some children do not wake when the bladder is full. They sleep through the wetting.

The NHS adds that bedwetting often runs in families, and that constipation and stress can play a part. The Sydney network puts it simply: bedwetting is not your child's fault.

What does not help, and what does

The UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guideline on bedwetting, first published on 27 October 2010 and last updated in August 2023, corrects several myths.

  • Punishment does not help. NICE says punitive measures should not be used. The NHS says punishing can make wetting worse.
  • Taking away rewards does not help. NICE advises against any system that penalises or removes rewards already earned.
  • Lifting does not cure it. NICE and the NHS say waking or carrying a sleeping child to the toilet does not lead to long-term dryness.
  • Cutting fluids too much does not help. NICE says enough fluid each day is important and asks doctors to check whether families are restricting drinks. Its suggested daily total for ages 4 to 8 is 1,000 to 1,400 ml.

On fluids, there is a fine line. ERIC suggests spreading drinks through the day and stopping about 1 hour before bed. The aim is better timing. The daily total should stay the same.

What does help, step by step:

  1. Drinks and toilet trips through the day. The NHS suggests toilet trips about 4 to 7 times a day, including just before bed.
  2. No caffeine. NICE and the NHS both advise avoiding caffeinated drinks.
  3. Reward actions, not dry nights. NICE says to reward agreed behaviour rather than dry nights, such as drinking well or a wee before bed.
  4. An alarm. NICE recommends an alarm as the first-line treatment when simple steps have not worked. A sensor sounds at the first drops, and the child slowly learns to wake or to hold on.
  5. Patience with the alarm. NICE says to check progress by 4 weeks and continue until at least 2 weeks of dry nights in a row. Bladder and Bowel UK says most children who respond are dry within 12 to 16 weeks.
  6. Medicine, if a doctor suggests it. NICE says desmopressin may be offered to children over 7 when quick, short-term improvement matters, such as before a school trip, or when an alarm does not suit.

The best evidence on alarms is a 2020 Cochrane review of 74 trials with 5,983 children. Children using alarms were more likely to reach 14 dry nights in a row than children without treatment. The reviewers rated the evidence as low quality and concluded that alarms may be more effective than no treatment.

Words matter too. Try: "Lots of children your age wet the bed. Your body is still learning. We will sort it out together." For sleepovers, a quiet plan with the host parent and a discreet pull-up in a sleeping bag can protect a child's confidence. That last point is our opinion, not a guideline.

The short version

  • Children aged 6 to 12 need 9 to 12 hours of sleep, according to the AASM and CDC. NIJZ in Slovenia says 9 to 11.
  • A 30-minute routine, the same wake-up time every day and screens off 30 to 60 minutes before bed are the main tools.
  • In 1 study, about 3 in 4 children aged 4 to 12 reported night fears. Calm, short check-ins are a good place to start.
  • About 1 in 5 children wet the bed at 5, and about 1 in 20 at 10. It is not their fault.
  • Punishment, lifting and cutting fluids too much do not help. Rewards for actions, alarms and a GP visit do.

When to ask your doctor or health visitor for help

  • Your child still wets the bed regularly after about age 6 or 7, or wets during the day too (Sydney Children's Hospitals Network).
  • Wetting starts again after more than 6 months of dry nights (NHS).
  • Your child is constipated, has pain or burning when weeing, or snores, which can be a sign of obstructive sleep apnoea (Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne).
  • An alarm shows no signs of progress after 4 weeks (NICE, Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne).
  • Nightmares are frequent, follow a frightening event or carry on after age 6, or night terrors start after age 5 or carry on after age 12 (NHS).
  • Your child, or you, are very upset by the wetting or by bedtime battles. The NHS suggests talking to your GP or school nurse. ERIC also has advice sheets and a helpline.
  • Same day: new bedwetting together with thirst, tiredness or weight loss. These are the 4 Ts of type 1 diabetes: toilet, thirsty, tired, thinner. The NHS says to ask for an urgent GP appointment or contact NHS 111. NICE says suspected type 1 diabetes should be referred on the same day.
  • Emergency: call 999 in the UK or 112 in Europe if those signs come with vomiting, tummy pain, fast deep breathing, drowsiness or confusion (NHS).
